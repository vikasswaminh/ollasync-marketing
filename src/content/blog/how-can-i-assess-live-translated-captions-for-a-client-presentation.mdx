## The short answer: test the whole client-facing screen

**Assess live translated captions by asking an external-language reviewer to follow a realistic, privately shared presentation on the same kind of device your client will use.** Show the deck, change slides, point at a chart, pause for the translated line to land, and invite a question. Have the reviewer report what they could read, what visual they were looking at when each line appeared, and which claim they understood. Repeat with a smaller window and with the actual guest permissions. A caption can translate a sentence acceptably yet fail the presentation because it covers the number in a chart, arrives after the slide has changed, or is invisible to the guest.

Use three separate decisions: **Can the guest access the feature? Can they read the slide and caption together? Can they still connect the message to the correct visual at presentation speed?** A fourth decision covers recovery: if any answer is no, can you pause, resize, restate, send an accessible text alternative, or move a consequential question to a qualified interpreter or reviewed written follow-up? Do not treat a polished host-side demo as evidence of guest-side access, and do not claim an accuracy percentage or accessibility certification from a short rehearsal.

This is deliberately narrower than our [live-translation preflight](/blog/how-to-test-live-translation-accuracy-before-a-meeting/), which tests whether numbers, negation, and commitments survive a client conversation. It is also different from choosing [translated audio versus captions](/blog/should-my-multilingual-meeting-use-translated-audio-or-captions/). Here the subject is the **presentation surface**: whether a person can watch a shared slide and read its accompanying translated text without losing either. Use the preflight guide as a companion for checking linguistic meaning, not as a substitute for this visual trial.

## What makes a presentation different from an ordinary call?

In a call without shared material, captions compete mainly with faces and meeting controls. In a presentation they compete with small labels, legends, annotations, cursor movement, a live product interface, and sometimes a second video or caption track. The speaker may say “the blue line here” just as the slide advances; the translated caption may appear over the next chart. A reader then has to infer which blue line was meant. Even if the words are correct, the combination is not usable.

Identify the presentation's purpose before judging any tool. Is the client supposed to understand the overview, inspect a numerical claim, evaluate a product workflow, or approve a proposal? For an overview, modest delays may be manageable with slower pacing. For a pricing decision or scope change, the team needs a reliable way to verify the exact statement and the client's response. The go/no-go standard should reflect what the guest must do, not how impressive the caption animation looks.

### Which output is the reviewer assessing?

Ask whether the guest is seeing text in the speaker's language or translated text in a selected target language. The [W3C media guide](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) calls same-language text *captions* and text translating speech into another language *subtitles*, although meeting products commonly label the latter “translated captions.” The distinction matters: a translated speech line does not automatically identify speakers or significant non-speech sounds. Some guests may need both language access and accessibility information; ask privately about the needed experience rather than assuming a translation toggle satisfies an accommodation.

For a broadcast-style live presentation, W3C's [explanation of live captions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/captions-live.html) describes speech and important sound information. It also explicitly says the criterion was not intended to make every two-way web call automatically subject to the same requirement. Neither statement certifies a particular meeting feature. If someone needs reliable captions as an accommodation, involve that person or an accessibility specialist in the rehearsal and consider professional live captioning where appropriate. Do not expect automated translated text alone to resolve every access need.

## How should you design a rehearsal that resembles the client view?

Create a short, non-confidential substitute deck with the **same layout patterns**, not the actual client's sensitive content. Include a title slide, a dense diagram, a chart with a legend and units, a slide with a quoted condition, a product demo or short shared video if one is planned, and an ending slide with next steps. Replace customer names and amounts with harmless examples but preserve the difficulty: small labels, color distinctions, jargon, and the speed of slide transitions. Use real prospective language directions and devices. A carefully rehearsed generic greeting does not test a pointer-heavy explanation of a chart.

Recruit at least one reviewer who reads the intended target language fluently and has not memorized the deck. A second reviewer can operate the guest device and take notes; in a small team, one person can do both, but avoid coaching them with the source script during the run. Explain the objective: they should say when the slide becomes unreadable, when a caption refers to an earlier visual, and which specific facts they cannot connect to the deck. Ask them to report what they saw **before** revealing what the speaker intended. For linguistic accuracy, use the separate preflight rubric with a fluent reviewer; the presentation worksheet below concentrates on visual access and timing.

Run the meeting with the actual host account, event type, meeting policy, screen-sharing method, and guest path. Invite a person using an external account, or a test guest whose permissions match the client's. Do not assume the internal co-host has the same controls. If an external guest test is impossible, record that access is **unverified**, not “passed.” Confirm permission to share any content and whether recording or a screenshot is allowed; a written observation log is often sufficient. Do not send client materials through a test vendor solely to evaluate captions.

### Which presentation scenes deserve deliberate stress tests?

1. **Opening and orientation.** Show the first slide and say who is speaking, the language being used, and how the guest can request a pause. Observe whether the translated line appears before you advance.
2. **Chart reading.** Point to one series, state its label and unit aloud, and ask the reviewer to find it while reading the caption. If the caption covers the legend, the reviewer should note the exact obstruction.
3. **Dense slide.** Present the smallest text you would normally use, then ask whether it remains readable at the guest's ordinary zoom level. Do not require the guest to make the whole slide tiny to fit more captions.
4. **Slide transition.** Finish a statement about slide A and move to slide B at your ordinary pace. Can the reviewer tell which slide the final translated line describes? Repeat with an intentional pause.
5. **Pointer or live demo.** Say a control's name and describe its action rather than relying on “this button.” Have the reviewer locate it while captions update.
6. **Question and interruption.** Invite the guest to ask about a visible claim; wait until their question has been understood before answering. Observe whether speaker turns and caption placement remain intelligible.
7. **Fallback scene.** Simulate losing translation mid-slide. Practice a pause, a plain-language summary, an accessible shared document, and a scheduled follow-up instead of improvising an unsupported promise.

Each scene tests coordination rather than generic word-for-word quality. If you cannot share a video clip because of audio-routing policy, mark that scene not applicable rather than interpreting silence as a successful caption result. If your deck includes live software, rehearse a real navigation path with synthetic data and test whether notification pop-ups or cursor tooltips compete with subtitles.

## How do captions, slides, and screen sharing interact?

There are at least three possible places text can appear: inside the meeting window as a personal overlay, in a separate caption pane, or embedded in a shared or recorded stream. The guest may be watching the presenter’s shared window while their caption controls belong to their own meeting window. Test what the guest actually sees; a caption on the presenter’s screen is not proof that it will be visible in the share. Conversely, sharing an entire desktop can expose private notifications or the host's caption pane unintentionally. Choose the share mode intentionally and verify it before presenting confidential material.

Check whether captions obscure the bottom of a slide, especially footnotes, totals, source notes, and chart axes. Move slide content away from the collision zone or select a supported caption position when the guest can control it. Do not put material terms in a tiny footer and hope the guest will zoom in while text is changing. Prefer a clean slide with one salient claim, a labeled chart, and a spoken explanation that repeats the visible label. A redesign can be more dependable than hunting for a platform setting that might only apply to the host.

Test the projected or room-view scenario separately. A client in a conference room may see a large screen but hear audio through room speakers; a remote reviewer in a browser may have personal captions. Ask IT whether the room display shows the meeting client's overlays, and verify with the actual output cable, display resolution, and room audio. If the room receives only a mirrored share, a personal caption stream may never reach that display. Provide an attendee-controlled link or a supported separate text display if needed, and confirm readability from the seating position.

### What counts as readable for the particular guest?

Ask the reviewer to set their normal browser zoom, display scaling, caption size, and contrast preferences first. Test the smallest window they are realistically likely to use, not an artificial minimum. Can they enlarge captions without losing the slide? Can they move or open the text without covering the evidence? Do lines wrap into a narrow column or disappear before they can be read? Is it obvious who is speaking when the client asks a question? Test with the actual script and device; a language with longer rendered phrases or different character shapes can change line wrapping without changing meaning.

Avoid asserting a universal font size or characters-per-second pass mark. The appropriate display depends on the person, device, language, and content. A practical review question is: **Could you read the claim on the slide and the translated explanation without pausing the meeting or guessing which part was referenced?** If not, change the content or pace and ask again. Record the reviewer’s answer as an observation, not as proof of conformance with a standard. Accessibility is an ongoing design obligation, not a badge awarded by one successful trial.

## How can you assess timing without inventing a latency benchmark?

A caption can arrive a little later than speech and still work when the presenter holds the slide; the same delay can be damaging if the screen changes immediately. Time the *relationship* between spoken claim, visible item, translated line, and slide transition. Note the moment the presenter names an item, when the complete meaningful caption becomes readable, and when the item disappears. You can note elapsed time using a clock or private screen capture if approved, but do not convert one rehearsal into a vendor-wide speed ranking. Network conditions, sentence length, language direction, and participant devices vary.

Use a simple scene log: “Slide 4 changed while the caption about Slide 3 was still forming,” or “Reviewer identified the series after a deliberate pause.” These observations lead to specific fixes. Leave the chart on screen longer, speak in shorter complete clauses, name the axis before citing the value, and wait after a material statement. Do not speak over a transition animation that competes for the same visual attention. Build a pause after every slide with a number the client must assess. Practice the pause so it feels intentional rather than like a technical failure.

Try a naturally paced first run, then a revised run. If the second run succeeds only because the presenter recites an unnaturally slow script that will not be followed on the real day, do not count it as solved. Give the facilitator a cue in presenter notes: “hold until the translated sentence finishes,” “invite clarification,” or “send chart detail after call.” Your reviewer can signal when the line lands; avoid asking them to agree to a contract or authorize work from a transient caption. For a consequential point, summarize aloud, obtain a clear confirmation, and send reviewed written language afterward.

## What should IT verify before inviting the client?

Have IT or the meeting owner check the following without requesting the client's credentials:

- Who is licensed or otherwise eligible: organizer, individual caption reader, or both? Is the feature included in the planned meeting type, not merely an event demonstration?
- Which policies or administrator toggles enable captions and translation? Can external guests use the relevant controls, and must they sign in or install a client?
- Who sets the spoken language and who selects each target language? If the speaker switches languages during questions, what setting must change and who can change it?
- Does the guest's browser, app, mobile device, or room system show text in a usable position? Can the user personalize size, contrast, or placement, and does that setting persist?
- What happens on reconnect, screen-share handoff, or breakout into a different event mode? Does the guest have to turn captions back on?
- Are captions saved, recorded, exposed in transcripts, or retained under this tenant's policies? Is separate consent required for a rehearsal recording? Do not promise confidentiality based on the absence of a visible caption history.

Use a test external identity managed by your organization where allowed, not a simulated role inside the same licensed tenant. Send the guest joining instructions privately and in their preferred language if possible. Include a non-technical fallback such as a call-in number, a text contact, or a reschedule path. A last-minute invitation to download software can be a more serious barrier than any translation error.

### How do current vendor documents inform the trial without deciding it?

**Google Meet:** [Google's translated-caption instructions](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/10964115?hl=en) name eligible Workspace editions and show attendee caption and translation controls. Google warns that some languages are rolling out and some are in beta, so a language list alone is not a guarantee for your meeting. Its help page also describes scrolling back through earlier translated captions while captions are enabled. That may help a guest inspect a missed line, but test whether scrolling leaves enough space to examine the shared slide. Confirm your edition, account, direction, and browser in the current help page and live guest session; do not infer availability from another user's screenshot.

**Microsoft Teams:** [Microsoft's live-caption instructions](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/meetings/use-live-captions-in-microsoft-teams-meetings) describe desktop appearance controls, the meeting spoken-language setting, and translated-caption license conditions. They distinguish meeting translation from organizer-preselected language options in town halls and live events. The documentation says an eligible organizer license can make meeting translated captions available to participants, while individually licensed participants may have their own translation option; confirm the exact entitlement and policy with your administrator. For a slide review, test whether the guest can position captions clear of the visual evidence; do not assume a desktop customization applies identically in a mobile or room view.

**Zoom:** Start with [Zoom Support's translated-caption documentation search](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/search?query=translated%20captions), then follow the current official article for your account and product. Verify eligibility, language pair, prerequisites for the spoken-language input, host enablement, guest selection, and whether the documented flow covers a meeting or the event format you intend to use. This is a **verification plan**, not a claim that every Zoom plan includes translation or that one platform has a fixed latency advantage. Test the client view just as you would for Meet or Teams; a caption feature list says nothing about whether your slide's footer remains visible.

**Any specialized multilingual provider, including OllaSync:** Ask for current product documentation on caption languages and directions, plan limits, display controls, guest onboarding, data handling, and screen sharing. Demand a real client-side trial rather than assuming that a marketing phrase such as “real time” proves usable timing. The same worksheet and decision criteria should apply to our product and competitors. No vendor should be marked as passing a presentation that has not been observed in the relevant account and guest environment.

These contrasts describe documented controls and questions, not a league table. Plans, regional availability, interfaces, and help pages change. Save the documentation link and the date of your check alongside the trial notes, and recheck before a materially different event. If the platform cannot provide the guest's required translated text path, select a different supported arrangement or arrange human language support rather than asking the client to pretend the captions work.

## Can you use a presentation-specific worksheet?

Copy this worksheet into your meeting plan. It records **what the guest experienced** rather than imposing a made-up score. Keep the notes free of client secrets. Mark each row *works*, *needs change*, *not available*, or *not tested*, and assign an owner and retest date to every unresolved item. A “not tested” row is not a pass.

| Scene or check | Guest-side observation to write down | Change to try | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Join and language controls | Could external guest find and enable the target language on their device? | Revise instructions, permissions, plan, or platform; retest externally. | IT / host |
| Opening slide | Could guest identify speaker and purpose while reading text? | Introduce roles aloud; hold opening slide longer. | Presenter |
| Chart or table | Could guest see label, unit, value, and caption at once? | Simplify chart; move caption if supported; provide accessible handout. | Deck owner |
| Slide transition | Did the final caption from the old slide arrive while it remained visible? | Add presenter cue and pause before advancing. | Presenter |
| Pointer or product demo | Did “this” or a moving cursor leave the guest searching? | Name the control and location; slow the walkthrough. | Demo owner |
| Guest question | Could guest read their own turn and the answer without misattributing it? | Moderate turns; repeat question; use interpreter if needed. | Moderator |
| Small window / room view | Was caption and slide readable at likely display size? | Change layout, separate display, or meeting format. | IT / accessibility contact |
| Material decision | Could guest explain the proposal without relying on a transient fragment? | Pause; verify meaning; send reviewed written confirmation. | Account owner |
| Feature failure | Did everyone know how to pause and switch to an agreed fallback? | Rehearse backup contact, interpreter, or reschedule. | Host |

Add fields above the table: **date; meeting type; host plan and tenant; documentation URL checked; source → target language; device and app/browser; screen size and display scaling; share mode; reviewer role; content version; guest sign-in status; consent/recording status.** Below it, note the first-run observation, the change made, and the second-run observation. Do not copy an internal access token, client name, or unreviewed proposal into a vendor support ticket just to fill a row.

### What would a worked example look like?

Imagine a fictional English-to-French client pitch on a laptop. The presenter explains a blue forecast line on slide 5 while pointing at a crowded legend. The guest's translated line appears as the presenter switches to slide 6. The reviewer correctly understands that the forecast rises, but cannot identify which of two blue series the speaker meant. The issue is not necessarily mistranslation: the caption, pointer, and visual reference arrived in different moments. Log “slide transition: needs change,” not “vendor accuracy: failed.”

The deck owner labels each series directly and enlarges the chart. The presenter replaces “this line here” with “the blue renewal forecast on slide 5,” then waits for the caption before advancing. On a repeat run, the guest identifies the right series while the slide is still visible. This works on the tested laptop, not necessarily on every device. Send a reviewed chart and written explanation for any approval.

## When should you change the meeting plan rather than keep tuning captions?

If the guest cannot read both slide and text after reasonable layout changes, move the decision-critical content to a reviewed document and use the meeting to discuss it at a manageable pace. If an attendee needs professional captioning, schedule it in advance; if interpreting is needed for nuanced negotiation, involve a qualified interpreter. If a platform cannot show the required language direction to the actual guest, choose another supported route. If the screen share includes sensitive information you cannot safely use in a test, use synthetic material with the same visual complexity and separately verify your data-sharing policy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I test with the final confidential deck?

Usually no. Begin with a synthetic deck that preserves the real visual challenges: dense legends, fine-print footnotes, transitions, and demo controls. Only use actual client material after confirming permissions, retention, recording, and data-handling requirements. A layout rehearsal can identify caption collisions without exposing commercial details. A final authorized dry run may still be useful, but it should follow the same access controls as the real event.

### Is a successful translation-accuracy preflight enough?

No. Correctly translated words can be unusable if a guest sees them after the referenced slide disappears or they hide a chart's units. Run the [meaning preflight](/blog/how-to-test-live-translation-accuracy-before-a-meeting/) for consequential statements **and** this presentation trial for slide-caption coordination. Neither test guarantees all spontaneous questions will work; keep a pause and clarification path available.

### Should I turn on captions in the shared window or on the guest's device?

Find out where the selected platform renders them. Personal caption controls may belong to the guest's meeting interface, whereas a shared screen may carry only what the presenter chooses to share. Test both the host and external guest views before making instructions. Avoid sharing a desktop with private notifications or unrelated content. If the guest needs a separate text display, verify that your meeting format and venue actually support it.

### What if the client watches from a phone?

Invite a reviewer to join from a comparable phone with the same app or browser route and typical display settings. Test a chart, a slide transition, and a question; ask whether the slide and translated text remain simultaneously usable. A small screen may justify a simplified mobile handout, a slower presentation, or a separate decision review. Do not infer phone usability from a desktop test or ask the client to inspect tiny financial figures during a live share.

### Can I use a recorded rehearsal to measure delay?

Only if participants and organizational policy permit recording and you understand what the platform captures. A private timing log can be enough: note which slide was visible when the complete line became readable. If you capture video, protect and delete it under your retention rules. One timing observation is not a general performance guarantee; use it to determine whether the presenter should hold a slide longer.

### Does a translated caption satisfy a Deaf attendee's access needs?

Not automatically. A translated subtitle may not contain the speaker and significant sound information needed in captions, and an automatically generated stream can be wrong. Ask about the attendee's preferred language and access path privately, test it with them when possible, and arrange appropriate captioning or other support. W3C distinguishes the functions of [captions and translated subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/); a vendor label does not erase that difference.

### What if the platform claims the language is supported but the guest cannot select it?

Check the account's eligibility, organizer versus participant entitlements, administrator policy, app version, meeting type, staged rollout, and source/target direction against the provider's current official help. Ask IT to troubleshoot with a test external guest. Mark the feature unavailable for this presentation until the guest actually sees it. Do not ask the client to attend solely to diagnose your configuration.

### What does a responsible go decision look like?

A guest on the intended access path can turn on the right language, read the slide and translated line together, follow references through transitions, ask a question, and recover if the feature stops. The owner has addressed every “needs change” or documented a safe alternative; material claims still get reviewed confirmation. If any critical condition remains untested, describe the uncertainty and choose a safer meeting plan. The result is a decision about **this presentation**, not a universal verdict on the technology.

## Make the presentation serve the reader, not the caption feature

Live translated captions are useful when the guest can locate the right words at the right moment without losing the visual evidence. That requires more than selecting a language menu: simplify the deck, test the external guest path, allow time for text to settle, and provide a way to verify material meaning. Keep the worksheet with the meeting runbook and retest when the deck, device, language direction, meeting mode, or plan changes. If the reader cannot see the claim and its translation together, change the presentation or its support arrangements before asking the client to decide.
