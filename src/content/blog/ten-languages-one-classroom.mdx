Every online teacher has the same invisible ceiling: the set of people who speak their language well enough to be taught in it. Remove that ceiling and the market for one good teacher stops being a country and starts being the internet.

Here's the practical tour of what teaching across our nineteen languages actually looks like — and where the honest edges are.

## The nineteen

**English · Spanish · French · Hindi · German · Chinese · Japanese · Portuguese · Arabic · Russian · Kannada · Tamil · Telugu · Marathi · Bengali · Gujarati · Malayalam · Punjabi · Urdu.**

That's the shipped list — the languages a learner can pick from during a live class, receiving your voice as translated captions or as spoken translated audio. Nineteen languages — ten of them Indian languages, from Hindi to Urdu, where a class taught in English and heard in Kannada or Tamil is the everyday case — covers the first language of a very large share of the online-learning world, and *any* participant can pick *any* of them: the trainer doesn't configure pairs, the learner just chooses. One session can run up to five different languages at the same time. Common technical terms that classrooms say in English — protocol, tool and product names — are kept in English inside the translation instead of being turned into textbook words.

## Three classrooms this changes

**The solo tutor.** An IELTS coach in Manila teaches conversation practice to students who chose Hindi and Arabic captions for the instructions, switching to English audio for the practice itself. The [tutoring use case](/use-cases/tutors) is the deepest version of this: your teaching quality becomes the product; the language barrier stops being the filter.

**The academy with global cohorts.** A certification course that used to run three times — English, Spanish, Portuguese — runs **once**. Same trainer, same quizzes, same replay; three markets follow live. The margin difference isn't subtle: one delivery instead of three.

**The distributed company.** All-hands training where the Tokyo office finally hears compensation policy explained in Japanese instead of decoding rapid English at 8am. Comprehension isn't a nice-to-have when the content is policy.

## What learners actually experience

Each learner controls their own class: pick a language, pick captions or spoken audio, change mid-session if they want. The audio option matters more than it sounds — reading subtitles for an hour is work; hearing a voice lets learners watch the whiteboard. Both arrive [a beat behind the speaker](/blog/how-live-voice-translation-works-in-class/), the rhythm of listening through an interpreter.

## The honest edges

- **It's machine translation.** Excellent for following a lesson; not a substitute for certified interpretation where the stakes are legal or medical.
- **Input quality is output quality.** A trainer on a decent headset in a quiet room translates dramatically better than a laptop mic in a café — exactly like human listeners, the recognition hears what you feed it.
- **Nineteen languages, not every language.** We list the shipped nineteen rather than promising the world, and a single session runs up to five of them at once. When that list grows, this page will change.
