## The short answer: make the terms visible, understandable, and correctable

To reduce mistranslations of names and technical terms in a live meeting, prepare a short, approved terminology sheet before anyone speaks. Include exact spellings, a plain-language definition, an approved target-language expression where a qualified reviewer has supplied one, and a note on what must **not** be translated. Put the important terms on slides or in a shared reference that participants are permitted to see. Ask speakers to say each unfamiliar term clearly, then define it in ordinary words. Assign a moderator to pause the meeting when a consequential term appears wrong, show the intended wording, and have someone who understands the receiving language explain the meaning back.

This procedure does not depend on a platform having a custom glossary. A document that humans can consult is valuable even if the captioning or translation system never reads it. Do not claim that pasting a word into a meeting chat trains a model, that uploading a file forces a live caption to obey it, or that a setting in one product applies to another. Where a vendor offers terminology controls, confirm the current feature, eligible plan, language direction, permissions, and actual live behavior in its official documentation and a rehearsal. Treat the control as an aid, not a guarantee.

The aim is not to make every sentence sound polished. It is to prevent a mistaken person, component, measurement, obligation, or next action from being treated as agreed. If nobody can verify the listener's understanding, defer a consequential decision to reviewed writing or a qualified interpreter. This article concentrates on **terminology management during the meeting**. For a general accuracy preflight, see the [live translation pilot guide](/blog/how-to-test-live-translation-accuracy-before-a-meeting/); for chairing a complete bilingual conversation, see the [bilingual meeting runbook](/blog/how-do-i-run-a-bilingual-business-meeting-without-a-human-interpreter/).

## Why do names and specialist words go wrong even when the rest sounds fluent?

A name may sound like an ordinary word. An acronym may have several expansions. A product code may be pronounced as letters in one office and as a word in another. A technical term may also describe a familiar concept outside the company, but with a narrower meaning inside it. In a live workflow, the source speech may first be recognized incorrectly; a translator can then produce a reasonable translation of the **wrong recognized words**. Alternatively, the original-language text can be accurate while the target-language term misstates the concept. The user sees one result, but the repair depends on identifying which meaning was lost.

Imagine an entirely fictional team discussing the “Cedar Gate” module. One participant hears “cedar gate” as a physical entrance; another knows it is an internal software component. Even perfect spelling does not communicate that distinction. Or the speaker says “Mira Vale owns the patch,” and a listener receives an expression that sounds like a place, not a person's name. Neither error is solved by asking everybody to speak louder. The team needs a shared reference defining *Cedar Gate* as the component and *Mira Vale* as the responsible person, plus an explicit way to correct the record.

People also compress speech when they know the domain: “RPO,” “release window,” and “rollback” may appear in one breath without expansion. When another language is involved, compressed phrasing leaves little context for a listener to infer which expansion was intended. Adding more specialized words to a slide is not enough if nobody explains them. A useful preparation process separates spelling, meaning, pronunciation, and desired target-language treatment. They are different questions with different owners.

Finally, the visual result can create false confidence. A smooth translated sentence may carry the wrong product name or turn a tentative test into an approval. Conversely, a caption can spell a name oddly while participants still understand the correct person from context. Prioritize errors by **consequence**, not by how strange they look. Correct a wrong unit, responsible party, negation, or contractual term immediately. Record harmless stylistic variations for later cleanup instead of derailing every exchange.

### Is a glossary the same as a translation feature?

No. Here, a **meeting glossary** means a human-readable reference approved for the audience. It can be a one-page PDF, an accessible shared document, a slide appendix, or a handout. A **vendor terminology feature**, if offered for the precise product and workflow you use, is a configuration that may affect a particular recognition or translation process. These have different access controls and different failure modes. The existence of the first does not prove the second exists; the presence of a vendor feature does not remove the need for speakers and reviewers.

Also distinguish an original-language transcript, same-language captions, translated subtitles, and spoken translation. They are different outputs. The [W3C guide to captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) distinguishes same-language captions from translated subtitles and cautions about automatic output. Checking the source-language caption, when available, may help identify a recognition problem; it does not itself validate the translation. A person competent in the receiving language must judge whether the intended concept came across.

## What belongs on the one-page terminology sheet?

Start with the items whose errors would make somebody ask the wrong person, order the wrong component, misunderstand a limit, or approve the wrong step. Do not dump the entire company dictionary into a meeting invitation. For a 30-minute technical call, choose perhaps five to fifteen relevant entries and revise the list if the agenda changes. The number is a practical starting point, not a tested accuracy threshold. Have the subject-matter owner decide which concepts are important and a fluent reviewer decide how they should be expressed for the actual audience.

For each entry, record: the approved source spelling; what kind of thing it is; a short description; pronunciation guidance only where helpful; whether it must remain unchanged; an approved target-language expression if one has been reviewed; a term to avoid if a plausible alternative would mislead; and who can approve a correction. Add a stable identifier, such as a slide number, if multiple items have similar names. Write “translation pending review” instead of inventing a target-language term. If a participant prefers a different spelling of their own name, ask them: an organizer should not unilaterally decide how someone's identity will appear.

A small fictional example shows the format. None of these entries describes a real product or an endorsed translation:

| Source label | What the speaker means | Treatment for this meeting | Check or owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mira Vale** | Person coordinating the demonstration | Preserve the person's preferred spelling; display on the introduction slide | Mira confirms spelling and role |
| **Cedar Gate** | Fictional software module, not a physical gate | Keep the product label; explain its function in ordinary words | Product owner approves description |
| **RTO** | Recovery time objective: target time to restore service | Say the expansion on first use; ask a regional reviewer for any target-language wording | Operations lead confirms scope |
| **Amber Queue** | Fictional holding state for unapproved requests | Keep the label in diagrams; describe what enters and leaves it | Workflow owner checks diagram |
| **12 ms** | Twelve milliseconds, not twelve seconds | Show both the number and unit on a slide; do not silently round | Engineer confirms measurement |
| **Draft 2** | A reviewable proposal, not a signed commitment | Label all copies “draft”; explain that approval has not occurred | Meeting chair confirms status |

This example deliberately mixes names, product labels, an acronym, a unit, and a decision state. A list containing only translations of nouns would miss some of the most consequential misunderstandings. If your real meeting includes code names, decide whether attendees are authorized to see them before circulating any sheet. A public placeholder used for rehearsal should never be confused with the live confidential reference.

### Should names be translated, transliterated, or left unchanged?

Ask what the name refers to and who has authority over its presentation. A participant's preferred name, the legal name of an organization, a registered product mark, and an internal shorthand do not have identical rules. On a written slide, retain the approved spelling when that is needed to identify a person or record; if a different script is necessary for readability, have the person or an authorized language reviewer approve the rendering. For spoken introductions, give a natural pronunciation cue when the owner wants one, but do not force someone to change how they say their name to accommodate a tool.

For a product with a descriptive English name, an explanation can accompany the fixed label: “Cedar Gate is the module that screens requests.” This is more reliable than hoping the literal words “cedar” and “gate” convey the module's role. For an acronym, say the letters, expansion, and purpose on first mention: “RTO—recovery time objective—the time we target for restoring service.” If the acronym has different meanings across teams, include a distinguishing context rather than choosing the first dictionary expansion.

Place the written identifier next to the relevant diagram and repeat it at a transition. “On slide 5, Cedar Gate receives the request” gives the listener an anchor even if live audio or subtitles vary. If you need a pronunciation note for the speaker, keep it in presenter notes; do not imply that a phonetic spelling entered into a human document will change machine output.

### Who approves translated terms when several regions disagree?

The person accountable for the underlying process approves the *concept*. A competent language reviewer for the audience approves the *wording*. Neither should silently overrule the other. A literal translation might be technically false; a technically precise term may be unknown in the receiving region. Resolve that tension with a description and a decision about what the participants must understand, not with a universal claim that one variant is always correct.

If the audience spans regions, keep the source identifier stable and add a neutral explanation. Note any regional term as a reviewer-approved variant, with the region and context. Do not label one participant's normal usage “incorrect” simply because another office uses a different expression. If there is no reviewer for an important language, mark the translated expression unapproved and choose a qualified interpreter or written review before asking for assent.

## How should speakers prepare slides and spoken explanations?

A slide can be a source of truth for spelling, units, and diagram labels, but only if people can actually access and read it. Share an approved copy before the call where permissions allow, number the slides, use selectable text rather than relying entirely on a screenshot, and provide an alternative accessible format when needed. A shared screen at an unreadable scale is not a terminology strategy. Put one critical name or concept next to its definition, not in a dense appendix that nobody can find during a correction.

Use a predictable introduction: **label → definition → example → check**. For instance: “Amber Queue is our temporary holding state for requests that have not been approved. On slide 4, the request stays there until an owner checks it. Does ‘not approved yet’ come through clearly?” The example and question add context without requiring the software to obey a custom term. Ask a fluent recipient to describe the meaning, rather than simply asking “Do you understand?” A yes may mean only that the audio was audible or that the participant does not want to interrupt.

Keep each sentence short enough for captions and interpretation to finish. Pause between a product name and the sentence that defines it. Avoid reading serial numbers at full speed or referring to “this,” “there,” and “that one” without pointing to a labeled item. Say “12 milliseconds on slide 6,” not “twelve, like we discussed.” Where the exact literal term matters, type it in a permitted channel and say, “Please use the written identifier on the slide.” Where the meaning matters more than the label, define it in plain language and invite a question.

Speaker preparation is not accent correction. Clear microphone placement, turn-taking, expansion of acronyms, and meaningful pauses improve the chance that people can follow a statement; no speaker should be told to suppress their accent or mimic another region. A moderator can ask a participant to repeat an important phrase, and the participant may choose a different explanation. Avoid making the person whose name is repeatedly corrupted responsible for all troubleshooting. Fix the process and provide a reliable written identifier.

## What permissions and privacy checks come before sharing the terms?

A terminology sheet may contain employee names, confidential product code names, unreleased specifications, client identifiers, or protected information. Decide who may receive each entry before emailing a deck, pasting it into chat, uploading it to a vendor, or adding it to a recording. The host should consult the organization's data handling rules and the specific meeting agreement; being invited to a call does not automatically authorize every attendee to receive internal vocabulary in advance.

Use the smallest useful reference. Replace confidential project names with approved public labels for an external exploratory meeting, or ask for permission to share the real identifiers when exactness is essential. Separate a public agenda from a restricted appendix; grant access only to the participants who need it. Check whether guests can open the document on their devices and whether accessible versions are available without exposing the restricted copy to a wider link audience. If you plan to send it to an interpreter, confirm the interpreter's access and confidentiality terms beforehand.

A human-readable sheet and a service-side upload are different privacy events. Before entering a glossary in any product feature, establish whether the product actually supports that input in the live workflow, what role may configure it, where the information is stored or processed, what retention settings apply, and whether your organization permits the submission. Do not use a transcription or recording option merely to diagnose one name without checking notice, consent, retention, and local policy. Where no saved record is permitted, write an approved correction and owner in a restricted action log instead of secretly capturing the call.

Permission also includes the right to correct. Let participants know at the start how to flag a mistranslation, who will pause the floor, and whether corrections can appear in shared notes. A guest should not have to challenge a senior speaker publicly to get their name or technical objection fixed. Offer a chat signal or moderator contact where appropriate, then bring the correction into the conversation so everyone hears the revised meaning.

## What is the fastest safe correction workflow during the call?

Agree on a brief signal such as “terminology check.” When someone sees or hears a suspect term, the moderator stops the current decision. The original speaker repeats the intended phrase once and points to its written reference. A subject-matter owner defines it without relying on the mistranslated word. A fluent recipient then explains what they understood in their language or in a shared language they are comfortable using. The group records the confirmed meaning and whether any previous statement needs to be withdrawn. Resume only after the ambiguity that matters has been resolved.

A compact sequence is **pause → identify → restate → read back → record → resume**. Example: “Terminology check. We said *Amber Queue*, the holding state for requests that are **not approved**. It is on slide 4. Could you tell us whether your team understood that these requests are still pending?” If the response describes approved requests, do not just repeat the label louder. Explain the process another way, show the diagram, and seek an independent explanation. If the recipient cannot verify the target-language meaning, stop treating that item as decided.

Do not edit the shared glossary mid-call without a trace when several parties depend on it. Add a dated correction with an owner and mark whether it supersedes an older term. For a material error, explicitly revisit any conclusion drawn while the wrong term was on screen. Saying “the captions were off” is not sufficient if the minutes already assign a task to the wrong person. Have the relevant owner restate the next action and confirm it with the other side.

### How can I tell whether the error happened before or after translation?

If the service permits viewing original-language captions, compare the written source words to what the speaker actually said. If the source caption already has the wrong name, the initial recognition may be the problem; provide the written spelling, improve the audio, and restate the sentence. If the source caption is correct but the translated result changes the entity or concept, use the approved description, correct the target-language wording with a fluent reviewer, and avoid assuming a source-microphone adjustment will fix it. If there is no source-caption view, do not guess at an internal cause from a single output.

Some services show live captions without preserving them; others offer separate transcription or recording settings. For example, [Microsoft's Teams captions documentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/meetings/use-live-captions-in-microsoft-teams-meetings) describes caption controls and distinguishes the live display from saved transcription. [Google Meet's translated captions help](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/10964115?hl=en) describes the translated-caption experience and eligibility. Neither link is a basis for claiming that either service—or OllaSync—ingests this article's working glossary or guarantees enforcement of a chosen term. Check the exact current product documentation and account settings before assuming a diagnostic view or terminology control will exist in your meeting.

## How do we confirm the record after a terminology correction?

At the end of each consequential agenda item, show a short statement with the person, object, action, status, and deadline. Read it aloud and ask the receiving party to restate the substantive point. Mark whether it is **agreed**, **proposed**, or **open for review**. “Mira will review Draft 2 by Thursday; no deployment is approved” is more verifiable than “Mira handles the next thing.” If a translation had previously indicated approval, say explicitly that the earlier wording was incorrect and should not be relied on.

Assign one owner to produce the written follow-up and a language reviewer to check the receiving-language version where necessary. Include the approved terms, a list of corrections, unresolved questions, and the slide or document version used. Send only through an authorized channel. Do not assume a machine-generated transcript is a reliable bilingual record: a wrong name can be repeated consistently, and a corrected spoken sentence may not replace the earlier mistaken line. If recordings are permitted, they can help investigate a disputed statement, but permission to record and ability to interpret the material accurately are separate matters.

When two terms remain unresolved, keep both labeled as provisional rather than silently normalizing one. The next meeting's speaker notes should include the correction and the context that made it necessary. If a recurring term was consistently misunderstood, ask the terminology owner whether the source wording itself should change. Improving a source explanation can be more effective than arguing over the perfect translation of an ambiguous phrase.

## Can vendor glossary controls solve this automatically?

Possibly some products offer relevant terminology settings for some workflows, but a feature name alone proves very little. A custom word list might influence speech recognition, a translation glossary might constrain text translation, and a human interpreter's preparation sheet might not be uploaded anywhere. Ask the vendor which stage uses the entries, which direction and output mode are covered, whether additions affect an already-running meeting, who can configure them, whether guests see the same treatment, and how to test that behavior. Ask for an official help page for the **specific live meeting feature** rather than a general marketing page for another product in the company's portfolio.

Only rely on a claim after confirming it in current official documentation and in a meeting configured like yours. Importing a glossary for document translation does not establish that it applies to live speech. Likewise, a product displaying translated captions does not establish support for custom terms. This article makes **no claim that OllaSync supports custom glossary uploads or deterministic terminology enforcement**. The human preparation, visible spelling, read-back, and correction workflow are useful independently of any such product capability.

Even a verified control cannot guarantee recognition when audio is clipped, two people overlap, a speaker uses a new acronym, or the listener lacks the domain context. It may be valuable to test a short list of approved entries in your actual configuration, but the rehearsal is a check on those conditions, not proof of universal accuracy. The [separate preflight article](/blog/how-to-test-live-translation-accuracy-before-a-meeting/) explains how to test language directions, meaning, and fallback decisions without promoting a polished demo into a quality guarantee.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can we prevent every name error by sending a glossary beforehand?

No. A human-readable glossary gives people a correction reference; it may never reach the software. A vendor control, where documented and available, may help some terms in some modes but cannot ensure every utterance is recognized correctly. The practical objective is to make important errors visible and recoverable. Put the exact names in an approved shared reference, introduce them with context, and agree that nobody will approve a consequential action while identity remains uncertain.

### What if one participant cannot see the slides?

Provide an accessible reference in an approved format and describe the identifier and meaning aloud. Do not make the visual slide the only route to a name or correction. The moderator can read a short term and its definition, send an accessible text copy if authorized, and give the participant time to consult it. Ask which mode they prefer; translated subtitles and same-language accessibility captions serve different purposes, as the [W3C explanation](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) makes clear.

### Can the meeting chair approve a translation without speaking the target language?

The chair can approve the business concept and decide to pause, but cannot verify target-language meaning by looking at fluent-looking text they cannot read. Ask a competent reviewer or the receiving participant to explain what they understood. If the outcome is consequential and neither can validate it reliably, defer to reviewed bilingual writing or a qualified interpreter. Automated back-translation can suggest a question to ask; it is not independent evidence that the listener understood correctly.

### Should we correct every typo in live captions?

Correct errors that could change identity, meaning, quantity, consent, safety, ownership, or a decision. Log noncritical spelling or style issues for later review when interrupting would cost more than it helps. If someone specifically asks for the correct rendering of their name, honor that request even if the chair thinks the typo is minor. Clarify to all participants whether the correction affects a prior statement or only the written display.

### When should we stop using automated translation for a terminology-heavy discussion?

Stop treating it as the basis for a decision when a material term stays ambiguous after restatement and read-back, when nobody can verify the receiving-language meaning, or when a misrendered term could cause harm or a binding misunderstanding. Move the disputed item to reviewed writing or arrange a qualified interpreter and relevant specialist. Even if routine context sharing continues, clearly label the disputed item unresolved; do not let the rest of the meeting imply agreement by silence.

## What should the team take into its next meeting?

A useful terminology process leaves behind a small, maintained reference rather than a vague promise to “improve the AI.” Keep the approved spelling and definition, the audience and permissions, the slide version, the reviewer and owner, and any correction that changed an action. Retire obsolete entries so speakers do not revive an earlier product label. Before the next call, ask the people who will listen whether the new wording makes sense to them, not just whether the list looks complete to the authors.

For ordinary multilingual meetings, the repeatable habit is straightforward: **prepare the terms, show them, explain them, pause on mistakes, and confirm the meaning before acting**. That is more dependable than assuming a vendor toggle will prevent every name or technical error. Where the stakes demand precise professional interpretation, use the appropriate human expertise and review process. A meeting is successful when the right people leave with the same understanding of the right objects and decisions—not when every live subtitle looks elegant.
