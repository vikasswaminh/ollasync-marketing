## Executive Summary: The Mechanics of Global All-Hands Delivery

Running a global all-hands meeting is fundamentally different from hosting a standard team video call. When an enterprise connects tens of thousands of distributed employees across North America, EMEA, APAC, and LATAM, structural challenges emerge around network bandwidth, firewall traversal, regional censorship, live multi-language audio interpretation, and engagement equity.

### 1. Enterprise Network Delivery & eCDN
The single greatest failure point of an all-hands meeting is the "local network crash." If thousands of hybrid workers return to office headquarters and simultaneously pull a 1080p unicast video stream through the corporate internet gateway, bandwidth saturates, resulting in widespread buffering. 

The top platforms solve this via **Enterprise Content Delivery Networks (eCDN)**—such as Microsoft eCDN, Zoom Mesh, Hive Streaming, or Kollective. An eCDN uses peer-to-peer (P2P) mesh routing or localized caching servers behind the enterprise firewall. Only a handful of video streams are pulled into the physical building, and the data is shared internally between endpoints, reducing office bandwidth consumption by up to 90–95%.

### 2. Edge Routing and Cross-Border Accessibility
A truly global company must service remote employees operating on residential broadband in EMEA, mobile networks in LATAM, and enterprise networks behind state firewalls in the Asia-Pacific region. Platforms like Zoom and Cisco Webex maintain globally distributed edge data centers with direct peering agreements across international internet exchanges (IXs). This ensures that an employee in Singapore or Sydney does not experience severe stream degradation due to transpacific backhauling to a US-based origin server.

### 3. Asymmetrical Interaction (Scale vs. Participation)
Traditional video meetings allow bidirectional video/audio, which becomes chaotic beyond 500 attendees. Modern global all-hands platforms use an asymmetrical architecture:
* **The Presenter Tier:** Studio-quality video, RTMP inputs, low-latency audio, and screen sharing managed within a dedicated backstage room.
* **The Attendee Tier:** High-scale, downstream-only broadcast (HLS/WebRTC) designed to preserve stability.
* **The Engagement Layer:** Out-of-band data channels powering live Q&A moderation, anonymous upvoting, live pulse polling, and native integration with platforms like Slido or native Q&A engines.

## 1. The Core Infrastructure: WebRTC vs. Low-Latency HLS vs. Hybrid Architectures

The first technical hurdle in global broadcast orchestration is streaming protocol selection. When determining **what is the best platform** for your engineering topography, protocol choice dictates latency, scale, and interactivity:

```
+-------------------+--------------------+--------------------+-----------------------+
| Protocol          | Latency Window     | Max Concurrency    | Bidirectional UX      |
+-------------------+--------------------+--------------------+-----------------------+
| Pure WebRTC       | 150ms – 400ms      | Low to Mid (&lt;10k)  | True Sub-Second Voice |
| LL-HLS / CMAF     | 1.5s – 3.0s        | Infinite (Edge CDN)| Moderated Chat / Q&A  |
| Adaptive Hybrid   | Sub-second to 2s   | 100k+ Tiered       | Dynamic Role Promotion|
+-------------------+--------------------+--------------------+-----------------------+
```

### Pure WebRTC Mesh & Selective Forwarding Units (SFUs)
* **How it works:** Real-time, peer-to-server-to-peer data channels.
* **The Trade-off:** While pure WebRTC allows any employee to unmute and ask a question with zero perceptible lag (&lt;300ms), SFU cascading across continents introduces severe compute overhead. Beyond 5,000 concurrent endpoints, connection negotiation drops packets across trans-oceanic transit pipelines, leading to audio jitter and frame drops.

### Low-Latency HLS (LL-HLS) with Chunked CMAF
* **How it works:** Video is ingested, segmented into sub-second chunks, and distributed via traditional Content Delivery Networks (CDNs).
* **The Trade-off:** LL-HLS easily scales past 100,000 simultaneous viewers with pristine 4K video. However, its 2-to-4-second glass-to-glass delay breaks natural conversational flow. It turns an all-hands into an executive television broadcast where feedback is relegated to text-based moderation queues.

### The 2026 Standard: Adaptive Hybrid Topologies
Modern tier-1 enterprise platforms employ an **asymmetric hybrid routing model**:
* **Presenters and Active Contributors (Tier 1):** Ingested via ultra-low-latency WebRTC pipelines routed through private cloud backbones (AWS Direct Connect or Azure ExpressRoute).
* **Passive Viewers (Tier 2):** Dynamically demoted to chunked LL-HLS feeds delivered by edge-cached distribution.
* **Dynamic Role Escalation:** When an employee in Singapore is called on to ask a question, the platform dynamically promotes their websocket connection from an LL-HLS subscriber to a WebRTC publisher in under 800 milliseconds.

## 2. Enterprise Network Optimization: eCDN, Mesh, and Edge Caching

A primary point of failure for global all-hands meetings occurs within physical offices. When 2,000 employees in a regional hub (e.g., London, Tokyo, or New York) join the same meeting stream simultaneously, they saturate the corporate WAN and local ISP gateway.

   +---------------+
      /    |    \

 [Peer] [Peer] [Peer] &lt;-- P2P LAN mesh shares segments locally
    |      |      |

Evaluating **what is the best platform** requires assessing its Enterprise Content Delivery Network (eCDN) integration:

1. **Peer-to-Peer (P2P) WebRTC Mesh:** Modern platforms deploy browser-based P2P data channels (via solutions like Hive, Kollective, or native WebAssembly agents). The first client in an office downloads a video segment from the external CDN; subsequent clients fetch that segment locally via the LAN. This slashes external bandwidth consumption by **up to 95%**.
2. **Server-Based Edge Caching:** For zero-trust networks where browser-to-browser UDP traffic is strictly blocked by micro-segmentation policies, the platform must support lightweight on-premises caching appliances (virtual machine or container-based) that ingest a single secure stream and fan it out across internal subnets.
