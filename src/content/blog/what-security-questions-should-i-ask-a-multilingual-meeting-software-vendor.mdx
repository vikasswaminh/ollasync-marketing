**Short answer:** Ask a multilingual meeting vendor to show where the *original speech, recognized text, translated text, synthesized voice, recordings and metadata* travel; which organizations and regions handle each; which parties can decrypt or retain them; and how your administrator can constrain and audit that processing. Then ask the vendor to demonstrate the precise combination of captions, translation, recording and end-to-end encryption (E2EE) your participants need. Request a dated data-flow diagram, subprocessor mapping, applicable data-processing agreement (DPA), retention schedule, key-custody explanation, identity-policy demonstration and a test using harmless sample content. An unanswered question is a procurement gap, not an implied security guarantee.

This is a *multilingual feature assessment*, not a general checklist for switching on AI notes. The related [IT-buyer transcription guide](/blog/what-should-it-buyers-ask-before-enabling-ai-meeting-transcription/) covers authorization, notes and transcript procurement in depth; [the recording notice guide](/blog/what-consent-and-notice-should-i-plan-for-recording-a-multilingual-meeting/) covers participant communication. Here the distinctive issue is that one sentence can become several language-specific streams, intermediates and copies, potentially handled by different processors. This is not legal advice. Privacy, employment, recording and accessibility obligations vary with the deployment, people and jurisdictions involved; have the responsible teams decide which rules apply.

## What multilingual service are we actually buying?

Begin with the *meeting scenario*, not a platform logo. Is the goal live same-language captions for Deaf participants, translated subtitles for a mixed-language discussion, interpreted audio, a multilingual replay, or searchable notes in several languages? These are different processing requests. A product may support one without the others; a feature may work in a live meeting but not in a recording, or only under a particular license. List every proposed language pair, endpoint and meeting type. Include mobile and browser participants, telephone dial-in, guests, interpreters and hybrid rooms, because a claim tested on a laptop may not hold elsewhere.

Ask who enables each feature: tenant admin, organizer, individual attendee or external integration. Can one participant request translated captions without the host creating a saved transcript? Can a host turn on voice translation for everyone? Does an invited bot join the call to obtain audio, or does the meeting service itself supply the language feature? Is the service producing *new speech in someone's likeness* or merely displaying text? Do not approve a vague category such as “AI translation”; document the version, plan and setting behind each outcome.

Scope the approval: “Internal training, three named languages, attendee-selected captions, no recording, managed guests.” Review higher-risk meeting classes separately.

### Is the translation optional for the participant or mandatory for the meeting?

This distinction changes both access and privacy. Ask whether turning captions off on a listener's screen stops processing their own audio or only stops displaying the result. Ask whether the host can force translation, whether other people see a participant's chosen language, and whether a participant can receive the original audio instead. Test mixed-language turn-taking, code-switching and a participant who joins midway. Record whether captions are private to each listener, broadcast to everyone or saved for the organizer. Do not infer retention from what is visible in the user interface.

## Can the vendor diagram each language-processing data flow?

Ask for a diagram for your chosen configuration that starts at a speaker's microphone and ends at every listener and retained artifact. Separate media transport from speech recognition, language detection, machine translation, text-to-speech, captions, recording, indexing and support diagnostics. For every step, ask: **what content arrives, who operates the component, in which region, what is logged, how long it stays, and who can read it?** Add the routes used when the language is unsupported, the recognition engine fails or the network reconnects. A single arrow labeled “AI” conceals the decisions you need to make.

For example, a harmless test utterance containing a fictional person's name could appear as source audio, a source-language hypothesis, a corrected transcript, a translated caption, a generated voice clip, a cached chunk, a recording track and an exported subtitle file. These are *possible* artifacts, not an assertion that every vendor creates them. Ask the supplier to mark which ones actually exist for the selected feature and which ones persist. Have them distinguish a short-lived processing buffer from an application log and a durable recording. If the vendor says “we don't store translations,” ask about retransmission queues, support traces, crash reports, backups, search indexes and outbound integrations.

Ask whether translated output inherits source permissions and whether a language switch changes its audience. If a transcript is translated *after* a meeting, who authorizes that separate processing event, and does it use another model, region or retention schedule?

### Which third-party AI processors actually see audio or text?

Request the current subprocessor list **and a feature-level mapping**. A list of hosting companies cannot tell you whether a speech-recognition provider receives audio, a translation model receives source text, or a speech-synthesis provider receives translated text plus a voice sample. Ask whether a processor can use inputs or outputs for model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, abuse monitoring or human review. The vendor's “we do not train on your data” statement does not necessarily bind an independent model provider or cover diagnostics. Obtain plan-specific written terms for each category of reuse; do not assume that “opt out” means no operational retention.

Ask how a new model provider or region is introduced, how much notice you receive, and whether you can object or disable the feature. Who answers a deletion request when the downstream processor has a separate retention window? If an AI API is used only when a particular language is selected, your vendor inventory should say so. For an especially sensitive use case, ask for a demonstration with egress logs or an architectural statement explaining how the supplier confirms the selected route. A subprocessor's own security badge cannot replace proof of what your tenant sends to it.

## Does E2EE still apply when captions or translated voice are enabled?

Ask which *specific* streams are end-to-end encrypted: meeting media, chat, screens, captions, translated audio and stored recordings need not share one encryption mode. Encryption in transit between a client and a service protects the connection but does not by itself prevent the service from reading content. E2EE means the service cannot decrypt the protected media in that mode; if a server-side translator needs the speech in plaintext, the vendor must explain how it gets it, or state that the feature is unavailable. Client-side translation might be possible under another design, but demand evidence of that design rather than assuming it exists.

Make the supplier show a live matrix of feature combinations. Switch on the documented E2EE mode, then try source captions, translated captions, interpreted audio, transcription, recording, dial-in and an external AI bot. Which actions disappear, fail, require the host to leave E2EE or start a separate service? Does the interface warn *every participant* when the protection mode changes? What happens if a late joiner uses an unsupported client? Ask whether the app can silently fall back to transport encryption. Save the product version and screenshots of the controls, but do not mistake a padlock icon for a complete key-custody explanation.

Microsoft's [Teams E2EE administration documentation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftteams/teams-end-to-end-encryption) explicitly lists live captions, transcription and recording as unavailable during its E2EE meetings; this is a documented Teams behavior, not a rule for all meeting software. Ollasync's [security description](/security) says its E2EE meeting key stays in participating browsers and that server-side recordings and AI on recordings are excluded from E2EE meetings. Test that product-specific boundary in your selected deployment. If a vendor claims *simultaneous* E2EE and cloud translation, request an explanation of where recognition and translation run and who obtains the plaintext and keys.

### Who holds the decryption keys, and for how long?

Ask about key generation, distribution, rotation, recovery and revocation for standard calls, E2EE calls, recordings and exports **separately**. Are keys held only on attendee devices, by the meeting provider, in a cloud key service, or under a customer-managed-key arrangement? Can a support engineer, escrow mechanism, integration or account recovery process obtain a usable key? Do linked invitations, browser history or forwarded URLs carry material that grants access? What happens when someone leaves the room or loses a device? A customer-managed key for stored recordings says little about who can see live audio during translation.

Request an architecture note and two-attendee mode-verification test. If E2EE excludes essential captions, plan a separately governed accessible alternative; never silently change modes.

## What exactly is recorded, exported and deleted?

Ask whether recording includes original speech, generated translated speech, captions burned into video, separate subtitle tracks, source transcript, translated transcripts, chat, screen share and speaker labels. Does the host choose which languages become part of a replay? Can an attendee's translated voice be exported independently? A searchable text copy can expose a discussion even when the video is access-controlled. Ask for a sample export using synthetic content, then inspect both file contents and permissions. A language-specific transcript may also contain names and timestamps that make an apparently anonymized translation identifiable.

Get a retention schedule **per artifact and copy**, including transient chunks, processing logs, main recordings, subtitle files, summaries, backups and downstream exports. Ask who controls default and maximum retention, legal hold, administrator override and deletion on contract termination. Does deleting a recording delete associated captions, translations and search entries? How long until backup copies expire, and can the vendor attest completion? An exported file in a customer's drive or CRM may fall outside the meeting provider's deletion control; document its owner and retention separately.

Test with a fictional meeting: record it, generate two language outputs, share it with a guest, delete it and search every permitted location. Then deactivate the guest and retry old links. Record timestamps for deletion from the primary application and any documented backup lag. Distinguish “no longer displayed” from “removed from all processing stores.” Avoid putting real employee or client material into a pilot merely to prove these controls.

### What notices and choices do speakers and listeners receive?

Ask the vendor to demonstrate notices for recording, live speech processing and AI-generated voice or translation, not just one generic “meeting is being recorded” banner. Is the notice available in the languages used by participants, readable by assistive technology, and visible to dial-in users? Does a guest arriving after translation starts see what is already enabled? Can the organizer pause processing when a sensitive agenda item begins? These product questions inform, but do not replace, your legal team's assessment of applicable recording and data-protection requirements. See the [participant notice planning guide](/blog/what-consent-and-notice-should-i-plan-for-recording-a-multilingual-meeting/) for the facilitator-side workflow.

## Where do content and people cross borders?

A buyer needs separate answers for **storage location**, **processing location** and **remote access location**. A regional storage setting may not constrain live inference, failover, diagnostics, subprocessor support, backup or exports. Ask for a region-by-region map for the selected language features and for the normal and disaster-recovery paths. Which country receives the speaker's audio? Which receives the source-language text? Can translated text travel to a different service than the recording? Where can administrators and support personnel access it? Include the recording host and the subtitle-export destination.

When personal data subject to the EU GDPR is involved, ask counsel to assess the applicable lawful basis, transparency, processor duties and international transfer mechanism. [GDPR Article 28 and Chapter V](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng) supply primary legal requirements for processor arrangements and transfers; an EU server label alone does not establish whether a restricted transfer occurs or which safeguards suffice. Ask the vendor for the operative DPA, annexes, subprocessor register and applicable transfer terms, then have your legal owner review the particular participants and route. Do not describe a product as “GDPR compliant” simply because it offers a DPA or a region selector.

Distinguish a participant receiving a translation abroad from a vendor processor handling speech abroad. If residency is mandatory, test each requested language and get a written list of unavailable features and approved alternatives.

## Does the DPA cover the selected language service?

Request the agreement applying to the *specific plan and feature*. Identify who acts as controller and processor for meeting content where the GDPR applies, and whether optional model services are separately governed. Ask which documented instructions allow live translation, generated audio, incident investigation and support review. Check the terms for confidentiality, authorized subprocessors, appropriate security, assistance with rights requests, breach notification, deletion or return, and audit information. These are topics addressed by [GDPR Article 28](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), not a claim that the offered contract meets it. Preserve the executed copy and the version of any incorporated online terms.

Ask whether the contract distinguishes content from telemetry and restricts voice-data reuse. Who approves a new model or transfer route? Do trial and integration terms differ? A DPA does not authorize recording every participant; meeting and legal owners must assess purpose and notice.

Treat assurance reports as bounded evidence: check the covered service, period and exclusions, including external translation APIs. No vendor certification or legal compliance status is asserted here.

## Can identity policy contain access to every language output?

Ask for a tenant demonstration of single sign-on, multifactor enforcement where available, roles, guest admission and offboarding. Who can create a room, switch on translation, initiate recording, download source audio, read translated captions after the meeting, share a link or invite an AI bot? Can an organizer override a tenant restriction? Are workspace admins able to revoke access for an external interpreter without removing all participants? Test an employee and a guest rather than assuming the same settings govern both. Record plan-specific limitations if an advertised control is enterprise-only.

In multilingual meetings, translated output can spread farther than original audio. Ask if a translated transcript is indexed under the same permissions as its source, whether a generated voice track inherits recording permissions, and whether a copied caption has a different owner. Test whether someone barred from the meeting can obtain a replay through a forwarded link or a summary posted in a chat channel. Explicitly assign ownership for exports outside the conferencing service. Device-sharing and unattended conference-room displays also deserve a practical check when captions are visible on a screen.

Check service accounts and integrations. Does an API token allow a bot to join meetings despite attendee restrictions? Can a CRM connector send translated notes to a broader audience? Ask for scopes, expiration, secret rotation and revocation behavior. Disable one pilot account and confirm whether its active sessions, download links and integrations still work. The desired result and acceptable delay should be agreed with your identity owner *before* the demo, not inferred from a vendor's SSO logo.

## What can we audit without creating another transcript?

Request an example audit trail for enabling translation, changing E2EE mode, starting or stopping recording, admitting a guest, exporting a language track, changing retention, accessing a recording and deleting content. Ask which entries include actor, timestamp, meeting identifier, target artifact and outcome; which are available to the customer; how long they remain; and whether the logs themselves include sensitive utterances. A security log that stores full caption text may become another unmanaged copy. Ask for redaction and role restrictions on log access.

Can audit events be exported to security monitoring? Simulate accidental translation in a restricted meeting: determine when it began, who accessed outputs, which processors received data and when containment finished. Record unanswered questions as limits on approval.

Use the [NIST Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework) as a voluntary way to organize identification, governance, control, communication and protection of privacy risk; it is **not** a certification or a substitute for testing. Assign an owner and review date to every material unknown. After rollout, recheck logs and feature behavior when a vendor adds a language, changes a model provider or alters meeting encryption options.

## Will accessibility survive the security configuration?

Ask people who need captions or interpretation to test the actual route. [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) distinguishes same-language captions from translated subtitles and warns about unchecked automatic output. Test speaker identification, significant sounds, screen-reader access, display options, accents and language switching with harmless content.

A security policy can inadvertently exclude someone if E2EE disables captions, or if restricting cloud processing removes their only interpretation channel. Ask the supplier to identify compatible modes rather than promising every feature works in every mode. Decide with accessibility and privacy owners whether a non-E2EE but tightly controlled session, human interpretation or another arrangement best serves a particular meeting. Explain the resulting tradeoff in advance and offer participants an accessible way to object or request an alternative.

For translated speech, test whether listeners can return to source audio and recognize errors; fluent output is not necessarily accurate. For replays, check whether edited translations retain an audit trail and inherit video permissions. See [translated audio versus captions](/blog/should-my-multilingual-meeting-use-translated-audio-or-captions/) for the participation choice.

## What belongs in the vendor evidence worksheet?

Use one row per proposed *meeting class and configuration*, not one score for the entire company. Keep a secure copy of sensitive architectural documents and a shareable summary of findings. At minimum, record the question, supplier answer, document or demonstration, document date, pilot version, owner, exception, decision and next review date. The following is a compact starting worksheet; replace “pending” with evidence or an explicit rejection, never with an assumption.

| Decision question | Requested evidence or test | Internal owner | Starting status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Which source and translated streams exist? | Dated per-feature flow diagram and synthetic utterance walkthrough | Architecture | Pending |
| Which AI subprocessors receive each stream? | Feature-to-processor map, reuse terms and change notices | Procurement/privacy | Pending |
| Which encryption mode supports each language service? | Key-custody note plus live E2EE/captions/voice/recording matrix | Security/accessibility | Pending |
| Where is each stage performed? | Normal, failover, support and backup region map | Privacy/legal | Pending |
| What survives meeting deletion? | Retention schedule and two-language deletion test | Records | Pending |
| Do the DPA and transfer terms cover this plan? | Executed terms, annexes, subprocessor list and legal review | Legal | Pending |
| Who can enable, export and share outputs? | Admin, guest, SSO/offboarding and integration demonstrations | Identity | Pending |
| Can we investigate accidental exposure? | Redacted example audit events and incident walkthrough | Security operations | Pending |
| Can all intended participants use the approved mode? | Accessible pilot across languages, devices and meeting roles | Accessibility | Pending |

Set gates before the sales call: no real-data pilot without agreed content routes and terms; no E2EE claim without a demonstrated language-feature combination; no rollout without access, deletion and accessibility tests. Record “approve,” “restrict,” “defer” or “reject” per meeting class, with a review owner. Use invented names in a pilot, test every proposed language, inspect exports, revoke a guest, delete artifacts and record any gap between documentation and actual tenant behavior.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a DPA enough to approve multilingual meeting translation?

No. It is one piece of the arrangement where applicable. You still need to know which speech and text streams are processed, where and by whom, whether the agreed terms apply to the chosen AI service, and how participants are informed. Have legal and privacy owners evaluate the meeting's purpose and jurisdiction-specific requirements; a standard document cannot validate your feature configuration.

### Can a meeting be E2EE and offer live cloud translation?

Not by assumption. A service-side translator generally needs access to speech or derived text; ask where plaintext is exposed and whether the mode still meets the vendor's E2EE definition. Microsoft's documented E2EE mode disables live captions and transcription. Other architectures require separate proof. Ollasync describes excluding server-side recording and its AI-on-recording path from E2EE meetings. Test the chosen mode and provide another accessible route when necessary.

### If live translated captions are not saved, is there no privacy risk?

No. Live processing still has input, output, potential subprocessors and operational logs. Ask whether the text is cached, sent to another model, shown to other participants or available in diagnostic systems. “Not saved as a transcript” is narrower than “never processed or retained anywhere.” Test the privacy setting and request a written description of temporary data handling.

### Does data residency guarantee there is no international transfer?

No. The advertised storage region may not cover inference, support access, backup or an attendee's exports. Obtain a route map for each language and fallback condition, then let the legal owner determine any applicable transfer mechanism. Neither a region selector nor a signed DPA alone guarantees legal compliance.

### What if a vendor will not identify its underlying translation model?

Ask for a confidential feature-level processor and region statement under an NDA, plus binding rules on reuse, retention and subprocessor changes. A proprietary implementation may limit architectural detail, but the buyer still needs enough information to assess the movement of meeting content. If material recipients and locations remain unknown, use only synthetic test data or defer approval.

### Should we demand a named security certification?

Follow your procurement policy and ask what service, period and controls any assurance report covers. A report does not prove that a specific translated-caption route, third-party speech provider, deletion operation or E2EE configuration is included. No certification is claimed here for Zoom, Microsoft, Google or Ollasync. Combine independent assurance with contracts, product documentation and direct tenant tests.
