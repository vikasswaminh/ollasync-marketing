A multilingual new-hire orientation checklist should answer a practical question: **can this person find, understand, and use the information needed to start work safely and confidently, in an accessible format?** Sending a translated welcome email is a start, not the finish. The useful checklist has named owners, dates, evidence of delivery, a way to ask questions, and a route for fixing misunderstandings without blaming the new hire.

This article is an operational template, not a substitute for local HR, accessibility, labor, or safety advice. Requirements vary by location and job. Adapt the policy and training items with the people responsible for those requirements; do not assume that a translation, a signature, or a meeting attendance record establishes comprehension. For the broader program design, see our [multilingual employee onboarding guide](/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-multilingual-employee-onboarding/). The checklist below focuses instead on what an HR coordinator and a manager can actually assign and verify during the first week.

## What should the checklist track for every hire?

Create one copy per hire or cohort. Before adding tasks, put these fields at the top: hire identifier; start date and time zone; work location and role; HR owner; manager; safety or site trainer where relevant; preferred language for orientation; preferred format for written, audio, and live communication; access or accommodation request contact; approved policy version and language; and next review date. Record a preference privately, allow it to change, and avoid treating a person's first language as a proxy for proficiency, disability, or reading preference.

For each item, use the same four columns: **owner, due date, completion evidence, and unresolved issue/next action**. A check mark means the assigned task happened; it does not mean the hire understood it. For high-consequence material, add a separate comprehension check and the name of the person who will revisit it. Store only the minimum personal information needed in systems your organization has approved. Avoid putting accommodation details or sensitive questions in a widely shared cohort tracker.

Use the sequence below as a copyable starting point. “Day before” means before the first scheduled activity, not necessarily a calendar day; move work earlier if shipping, scheduling interpreters, or reviewing specialized materials takes longer. An item stays open if a link exists but the hire cannot access it, or if the translated version does not match the current source. The goal is a usable first week, not a perfectly green dashboard.

## What belongs on the day-before checklist?

### Confirm language and communication access

- [ ] **HR owner:** Ask the hire which language or languages and formats they want for orientation materials and live questions. Offer a private reply path and make clear that preferences can be updated; do not require the hire to explain a disability to a group.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Send an agenda with local start time, time zone, session length, breaks, location or join instructions, and a named contact if the connection fails. Explain which sessions are live, which can be completed later, and how to request a different format.
- [ ] **Content owner:** Inventory the exact documents the hire will need: welcome schedule, workplace rules, required policies, payroll and benefits instructions, safety procedures, role-specific guides, and help contacts. Label each file with its version and review date.
- [ ] **Content owner:** Match each priority document to an approved language version. Mark untranslated or outdated items as open, assign a reviewer and due date, and provide a supported explanation path rather than silently substituting machine output for approved text.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Test the live session from a participant account: language selection, captions, audio, keyboard navigation, mobile view where relevant, and the route for asking a private question. Confirm the fallback if interpretation or the network fails.

Ask **“Which language would make these instructions easiest to use?”**, not “Do you speak English?” Some people want a translated overview but the original policy text; others want captions in the spoken language plus a written summary in another. Keep the approved source alongside localized versions, identify which version governs internal decisions, and let HR resolve discrepancies. If no suitable language access is ready for a critical session, reschedule that portion or arrange qualified human support rather than calling a generic auto-translation a completed orientation.

List acronyms, equipment labels, and safety signals with plain-language explanations and reviewed translations. Localize contact details as well as words.

### Prepare access and practical logistics

- [ ] **IT or site owner:** Confirm device delivery or workstation readiness, identity verification through the organization's normal process, sign-in instructions, authentication setup, and a reachable support route. Never send passwords in the orientation document.
- [ ] **IT and HR:** Verify the hire can open the actual documents, meeting, learning portal, and help channel from the device they will use. “Access granted” is not evidence that the file opens or that the language option is visible.
- [ ] **Manager:** Identify a named buddy or contact who can help with navigation without being expected to interpret confidential or safety-critical material. Give both people the scope and time for that help.
- [ ] **Site or remote coordinator:** Share arrival instructions, accessibility information, emergency contact path, and any equipment or connectivity needs applicable to the role. Confirm who will meet the hire and how to reach them if plans change.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Check the calendar for time-zone conflicts, religious or local holidays where known, and uninterrupted breaks. Offer an alternate session where scheduling excludes someone rather than recording them as absent.

### Review safety and high-consequence material first

- [ ] **Safety owner and manager:** Identify the hazards, emergency instructions, reporting routes, and training that apply to this specific work and location. Ask the appropriate local expert what must occur before the hire starts particular tasks.
- [ ] **Safety owner:** Review translations of safety-critical terms, labels, diagrams, and demonstrations with someone competent in the topic and language. Record who checked which version; do not rely on a single automated caption or subtitle pass.
- [ ] **Manager:** Block assignments that depend on unfinished training or authorization until the responsible owner clears them. Put the hold and the path to release in the manager handoff.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Prepare a practice scenario—such as finding the incident reporting route or explaining when to stop a task—that checks understanding without turning the exercise into a language fluency test.

The needed safety content depends on the actual job, jurisdiction, and site. Do not invent a universal list of mandatory training. A home-based software hire and a machinery operator need different walkthroughs. Whenever a person must act on an instruction under pressure, prioritize accurate language support and a practical demonstration over a long, generic orientation video. Flag conflicting localized instructions before the hire is exposed to the task.

## What belongs on the day-one checklist?

### Open with a navigation check, not a lecture

- [ ] **HR facilitator:** Welcome the hire, confirm pronunciation and name preference without putting them on the spot, explain the agenda, and show how to turn on language and accessibility options.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Ask the hire to open the agenda, switch the caption or language setting if desired, and send a test question through the agreed channel. Provide a phone or alternate route for technical failure.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Explain that questions can be asked in the preferred language and privately; allow pauses for interpretation and written responses. Do not ask a cohort to disclose personal accommodation needs.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Introduce each contact by role: HR for employment and benefits questions, manager for priorities, IT for access, and the safety owner for hazards. Give a single place where contact details can be found later.

A fast opening check reveals problems before the most important content begins. If the hire cannot see captions, find the policy, hear the facilitator, or submit a question, pause and fix the path. The same-language caption track and the translated subtitle track solve different needs. W3C's [captions and subtitles guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) distinguishes captions that convey speech and relevant non-speech audio from translated subtitles; a translated speech track alone is not a complete replacement for accessibility captions. Test the actual output, not just the presence of a toggle.

### Deliver policies and acknowledgments deliberately

- [ ] **HR or policy owner:** Provide the current, approved policy documents in the available agreed formats and explain purpose, where to find them later, and whom to contact about ambiguity.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Walk through decisions the hire may have to make—such as requesting time off, reporting an issue, or finding a benefit contact—using the actual workflow and a plain-language example.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Record delivery of each document and its version separately from any requested acknowledgment. Provide time to read and a way to ask questions before collecting an acknowledgment.
- [ ] **HR owner:** If the hire identifies a mismatch between language versions or says a document is not understandable, mark the acknowledgment as pending, refer it to the policy owner, and arrange an accessible clarification. Do not ask a buddy to certify an official interpretation.

An acknowledgment can show that a document was received or a workflow completed, depending on your own policy; it cannot prove understanding by itself. Avoid blanket statements such as “all legal forms must be signed on day one.” Your HR or legal team must determine which records, notices, and timelines apply. Keep the original document, translated version, version identifiers, questions, answer owner, and eventual disposition traceable in your authorized system. Do not turn a translation defect into a hire's missed deadline without escalating it.

If a live walkthrough uses slides, send a readable version in advance or afterward. Include full links and useful labels rather than screenshots of tiny text. When a form requires input, label its fields and say what belongs in them. W3C's [guidance on labels and instructions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/labels-or-instructions.html) explains why identifying inputs matters; the checklist should verify that a hire can actually complete the form, not merely locate it.

### Check safety understanding and the first task

- [ ] **Safety or site trainer:** Show the applicable emergency route, stop-work or escalation contact, location-specific hazards, protective equipment or setup where relevant, and the method for reporting a concern. Use the approved material and a qualified explanation path for critical details.
- [ ] **Trainer:** Ask the hire to demonstrate where to find help or describe what they would do in a plausible situation. Correct the instruction or repeat the demonstration if the response exposes a gap; document the follow-up, not a punitive score.
- [ ] **Manager:** Give one bounded first task with its purpose, expected output, due time, success criteria, and a safe escalation route. Confirm permissions and necessary training before assigning work that requires them.
- [ ] **Manager:** Ask the hire to explain the next step in their own words or preferred language and invite clarification. Record blockers such as missing access, unclear terms, or an unreviewed procedure.

This is a **teach-back-style check**, not a request to recite a script. “Where would you go if the alert appears?” yields more useful evidence than “Do you understand?” A correct answer to a quiz in a translated slide deck is not enough if the hire cannot find the same instruction on the floor or in the real system. For remote hires, practice finding the online incident route or help contact from their own device. For site hires, physically locate the relevant instructions where possible.

### Close with a real handoff

- [ ] **HR owner:** Send the manager a short handoff: completed items, approved language and format choices needed to support work, pending policy questions, access defects, safety holds, and next owners. Exclude confidential accommodation specifics unless sharing is authorized and necessary.
- [ ] **Manager:** Confirm receipt, acknowledge any task restrictions, and schedule the next check-in. Do not assume an HR meeting covers team-specific practices.
- [ ] **Hire:** Receive a plain-language recap with the next meeting, contact list, outstanding items, and a clear route to report a mistake in the recap. Offer the chosen language or a supported alternate format.

Day one ends when the hire knows what happens next and the manager knows what remains open—not when the last slide is presented. A single issue register prevents an unresolved language problem from disappearing between HR, IT, and the team. Assign each blocker to a person and a date. If the blocker is safety-related, keep the relevant task on hold until the appropriate owner resolves it.

## What belongs on the week-one checklist?

### Turn orientation into supported practice

- [ ] **Manager:** Break the role into a small set of first-week tasks. For each, provide the approved instruction, a demonstration or example, a success criterion, and a channel for questions in the hire's agreed format.
- [ ] **Buddy or designated peer:** Show how to navigate routine tools and team conventions; refer formal policy, confidential, and safety questions to the correct owners.
- [ ] **Trainer:** Run practice or observation on the tasks where misunderstanding would matter most. Revisit explanations using a different example or modality if the first pass did not work.
- [ ] **Manager:** Check whether translated terms match the labels the hire sees in actual software, equipment, and signage. Record any mismatch for the content owner.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Follow up on open access, policy, accessibility, or accommodation requests through the approved private channels. Confirm the hire knows when to expect an answer.

New hires need permission to ask the same question after the meeting. Set a short regular check-in and offer asynchronous questions when schedules or language processing make live calls difficult. Do not equate silence with understanding; check the task, not the person's willingness to interrupt a room. If the hire prefers to draft questions in one language and receive a response in another, agree on a process and confirm important details back with them.

Use practice artifacts that resemble real work: completing a sample request, finding a specific safety instruction, identifying the owner of a customer handoff, or submitting a test support ticket. For confidential systems, use a safe sandbox or demonstration account rather than real employee data. A facilitator can observe whether the instruction was usable without recording a personal profile of language errors. Where a role needs formal certification or sign-off, use the applicable internal process; the first-week checklist should point to it, not substitute for it.

### Check accessibility alongside translation

- [ ] **Content owner:** For each prerecorded orientation video, review synchronized captions for accurate speech, speakers, and meaningful non-speech sound. Review translated subtitles separately for terminology and timing.
- [ ] **Content owner:** Provide a usable text alternative or transcript where needed, including essential visual information rather than only the spoken words. Make the document searchable and readable without audio.
- [ ] **IT or accessibility owner:** Test keyboard operation, focus order, screen-reader announcements, readable zoom, and language identification in the actual portal and document flow used by the hire.
- [ ] **Facilitator:** Confirm a live-session alternative for people who cannot use a particular modality; do not assume live machine captions, translated audio, or recordings will meet every need.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Provide a private accommodation request channel and track the service request to resolution without exposing its details in the shared checklist.

Translation and accessibility overlap but are not interchangeable. W3C explains that [captions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) convey audible information, while [transcripts](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/transcripts/) can make audio content available as text and descriptive transcripts can include necessary visual information. Its [WCAG guidance for prerecorded captions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/captions-prerecorded.html) describes captions for synchronized prerecorded media; this is a useful design reference, not a claim that every employer is subject to the same legal standard. For multilingual web pages, identifying the [language of the page](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/language-of-page.html) helps assistive technology choose appropriate pronunciation rules. Ask the accessibility owner to evaluate the whole path, including forms and playback controls.

### Gather feedback without forcing disclosure

- [ ] **Manager:** Ask three concrete questions: “Which instruction was easiest to use?”, “Where did you get stuck?”, and “What would make the next task clearer?” Offer a written or private response route.
- [ ] **HR owner:** Ask whether the promised language and formats were available at the moment they were needed, whether the hire knew whom to contact, and whether any answer remains outstanding.
- [ ] **Content owner:** Triage feedback into inaccurate translation, inaccessible format, missing context, outdated source, workflow friction, and scheduling or access failure. Fix the source when the problem is in the source.
- [ ] **Manager and HR:** Agree who will communicate each correction to the hire and when. Check back after the fix; a closed support ticket is not proof that the instruction now works.

## Who owns each part of the checklist?

A simple ownership map prevents “multilingual” from becoming nobody's job. **HR** owns the agenda, welcome path, policy delivery, acknowledgment workflow, and private follow-up. **The manager** owns role expectations, work permissions, first tasks, and the continuing check-in. **A safety or site expert** owns location- and task-specific hazard instruction and its clearance. **The content or localization owner** maintains versioned source material, terminology review, and translated assets. **IT or the accessibility owner** tests account access and the usability of the delivery path. A **buddy** supports informal navigation, not official interpretation. The **hire** gets a clear way to raise problems but does not own fixing broken materials.

For a small organization one person may hold several roles. Keep the responsibilities distinct even if the names repeat. Use a handoff table in your existing HR system or tracker with columns for item, owner, evidence, due date, blocker, and resolver. A workable handoff might read: “Emergency procedure demonstration: site trainer; due before equipment use; demonstration pending because the local-language diagram is outdated; content owner reviewing diagram; manager holds equipment assignment.” That tells the next person what to do. “Training incomplete” does not.

Set escalation rules in advance: a safety conflict goes to the site expert and manager; a policy mismatch goes to its policy owner; a failed login goes to IT; a format barrier goes to the accessibility contact. Name a backup when the primary owner is away. Give the hire one front door for help even if the internal resolution route has several specialists. The internal route should be visible to owners, not an obstacle course for the new employee.

## How should live translation fit without replacing review?

Live interpretation, translated captions, and translated audio can make a shared orientation session easier to follow; they do not validate policy text or guarantee that a hazard instruction was understood. Choose the delivery method by risk. A casual team introduction may tolerate rough edges and a clarification afterward. A safety demonstration or consequential policy explanation needs the qualified owner to verify the language path and the hire's ability to act on the instruction. Preserve a human escalation option for ambiguities.

For a platform example, [Ollasync's current live-translation feature description](/features/live-translation/) describes participant-selected translated captions and optional spoken audio in a live session, plus same-language captions. Those are features to test against the actual languages, devices, and meeting setup of your cohort—not a substitute for a professional interpreter, a reviewed document, or an accommodation assessment. The feature description also notes that translation is not available in end-to-end encrypted meetings and that simultaneous language choices have a product limit. Confirm present capabilities and terms before procurement or rollout; do not promise a feature on the checklist that the hire's session cannot use.

The role page for [HR global onboarding](/roles/hr-global-onboarding/) describes delivering a shared orientation across regions. That can simplify scheduling, but the accountable workflow remains the same: send materials in advance, test access for each participant, leave time for questions, provide an alternate path if the live language channel fails, and carry unresolved items into the manager handoff. For more on instruction beyond the first week, see [training employees who do not primarily use English at work](/blog/how-to-train-non-english-speaking-employees-effectively/).

## How do you measure whether orientation worked?

Use a small set of operational measures, not unsourced retention promises. Define the denominator and review period before reporting any rate. **Access readiness:** among hires scheduled to start, how many could open the agenda, materials, and live session before the first activity? **Language delivery:** among requested language or format options, how many were available and tested when needed? **Critical instruction follow-through:** for applicable safety or job tasks, how many hires completed an observed demonstration or approved role-specific check before doing the work? A missed check remains pending; attendance should not count as success.

Also track **open blockers at day-one close** and their age, **time to resolve policy or translation questions**, **version discrepancies found and corrected**, and **first-week follow-up completion**. Record counts and patterns by process or content version rather than publishing small groups that could identify an individual. Segment carefully by site, role, or delivery path when it helps locate a fix; do not interpret a language group with more reported issues as a less capable workforce. It may mean that group finally has a usable way to report defects.

## What mistakes should the checklist prevent?

**Mistaking delivery for comprehension.** A sent PDF, played video, or checked acknowledgment proves an action occurred, not that instructions were usable. Pair important items with a concrete practice step and a route to clarification.

**Using one machine translation for every level of risk.** A draft may help someone navigate an agenda; an ambiguous safety instruction or consequential policy needs a competent review and an owner who can answer questions. Prioritize review by impact and keep a hold on affected work until the right person resolves the gap.

**Treating translated subtitles as accessibility captions.** The two serve different needs. Preserve same-language captions that include relevant sound information, review translations separately, and offer text and assistive-technology-compatible alternatives. Do not infer an individual's access needs solely from their preferred spoken language.

**Overloading day one.** Move setup, glossary, and nonurgent reading before the meeting or into the week where appropriate. Reserve live time for navigation, meaningful questions, high-consequence demonstrations, and introductions to real people. Schedule breaks and a recap.

**Closing HR's ticket before the manager receives it.** The hire may leave orientation with a missing account, a pending policy answer, or a task restriction. Send a written handoff, assign each open issue, and confirm the manager's next check-in. The checklist has failed if nobody owns tomorrow.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every document need to be translated before the first day?

Not necessarily. Start with what the hire needs to make decisions, complete required steps, and work safely; identify the approved source and arrange a suitable explanation path for anything not yet localized. The responsible HR, policy, and safety owners should decide which items cannot wait. Mark gaps openly, do not present an unreviewed draft as approved, and keep dependent tasks on hold where appropriate. Build a review queue for lower-priority material instead of pretending the entire library can be localized overnight.

### Can a bilingual teammate interpret the orientation?

A teammate may help with informal navigation if both people agree and it fits their role. Do not assume bilingual ability qualifies someone to interpret confidential employment information, resolve policy ambiguity, or deliver safety-critical training. In those situations ask the responsible owner to arrange appropriate language support and verify the meaning. Give the buddy a clear referral path so they do not become the default answer to every question.

### Is an acknowledgment enough evidence that the hire understood a policy?

No. Keep delivery, acknowledgment, questions, and demonstrated understanding as distinct records where each matters. Explain what the acknowledgment means in your process and provide time to review the document. If the hire reports a language or format barrier, route it to the policy owner and record the resolution before treating that item as settled. Ask local advisers which forms and records apply; a generic checklist cannot set legal deadlines.

### What if a requested language is not supported in a live session?

Tell the hire before the session if possible. Arrange a qualified alternate interpretation or a different format or time appropriate to the material; confirm that the alternate works and provide the same chance to ask questions. Do not force attendance at an inaccessible session merely to meet a completion target. Flag any safety- or policy-dependent work to the manager until the hire has a usable path through that content.

### What should a manager do after HR orientation ends?

Review the handoff, respect any training or access holds, introduce the first bounded task, and run a check-in during the week. Ask the hire to show where they found the relevant instruction and how they would escalate a blocker. Follow up on open language and accessibility issues with their assigned owners rather than improvising policy answers. Confirm the hire receives the corrected instruction, not only a notification that a ticket closed.

## Where should the first improvement start?

Copy the three sections into your existing tracker. Test them with one hire: language choice, document access, orientation, safety, policy questions, first task, and handoff. Give each failure an owner and next action. Review feedback after week one and revise before the next start.
