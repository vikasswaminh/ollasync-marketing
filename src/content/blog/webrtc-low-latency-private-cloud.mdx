For most people, a video call either "feels fine" or "feels laggy," and that's the entire vocabulary they have for it. There's no dial in the UI that says "184 milliseconds," no indicator that quietly confesses the SFU is three time zones away from half the participants. The call either flows the way a real conversation flows — where you can jump in, interrupt, finish someone's sentence — or it turns into the stilted, overlapping, "sorry, you go first, no you" experience that makes everyone tired after forty minutes for reasons they can't quite name.
That gap between "fine" and "laggy" is razor thin in absolute terms. Human beings start noticing delay in a conversation at around 150–200 milliseconds of round-trip lag — not because we're especially sensitive instruments, but because that's roughly the threshold where the rhythm of natural turn-taking breaks down. Push past 300 milliseconds and a call stops being a conversation and starts being two people taking turns broadcasting at each other. The entire discipline of low-latency video engineering exists to keep every call on the right side of that line, across every unpredictable combination of home Wi-Fi, mobile network, corporate firewall, and — increasingly — the specific infrastructure decision of where the video actually gets relayed through.
This is where WebRTC comes in, and where private cloud architecture starts to matter more than most procurement conversations give it credit for. WebRTC is the open standard that makes real-time video possible directly in a browser, no plugin required. Private cloud is the decision to run the infrastructure via [self-hosted video conferencing](/self-hosted) somewhere you control, rather than renting it from a vendor's shared, multi-tenant cloud. Put those two things together correctly, and you get calls that are both fast and yours. Get the second half wrong, and you can build something that's more secure on paper and noticeably worse to actually use — which is precisely the trap this piece is trying to help you avoid.

## Why "It Feels Laggy" Is Really Six Separate Problems

Before touching a single line of WebRTC configuration, it's worth breaking "latency" into the pieces engineers actually have to optimize separately, because a fix for one stage does nothing for the others — and a lot of wasted engineering effort comes from tuning the wrong stage.
Capture delay is the time between a camera sensor recording a frame and that frame being handed to the encoder — typically small, a handful of milliseconds, but not zero, and worse on lower-end hardware or when a device is under other load.
Encode delay is the time the codec spends compressing that raw frame into something small enough to transmit. This is where a meaningful chunk of controllable latency lives, because encoders trade off compression efficiency against speed — squeeze harder for a smaller file, and you pay for it in processing time.
Network delay is everything that happens between the sending device and the receiving device hitting the wire — propagation delay determined by physical distance and the speed of light in fiber, plus queuing delay at every router and switch in between, plus the retransmission delay when packets get lost and need recovering.
Relay delay is the time added by any server sitting in the middle — and in group video calls, there almost always is one. This is the stage that infrastructure decisions, including self-hosting, most directly influence, and it's the one this article spends the most time on.
Decode delay mirrors encode delay on the receiving end: unpacking the compressed stream back into raw frames the display can show.
Render delay is the final, often-overlooked stretch — buffering frames briefly to smooth out network jitter before painting them to screen, because rendering every frame the instant it arrives, jitter and all, looks worse than holding a tiny buffer and rendering smoothly.
Add all six together and you get what a user actually experiences as "lag." The uncomfortable truth is that most of the popular conversation about video-call latency focuses obsessively on one or two of these — usually network and relay — while ignoring that a poorly tuned encoder or an overly conservative render buffer can quietly cost you as much delay as an ocean crossing does.

## What WebRTC Actually Is, Underneath the Marketing

WebRTC (Web Real-Time Communication) gets described in marketing copy as "the technology that powers browser video calls," which is true but tells you almost nothing about why it's good at the job. It's actually a bundle of several distinct, individually mature protocols, standardized jointly by the IETF and W3C, wired together to solve a genuinely hard problem: getting real-time audio and video directly between two devices that have never met, sitting behind two different home routers, on two different ISPs, without either party installing anything.
Strip away the acronyms and the bundle breaks into four jobs:
Getting media. The getUserMedia API grants a web page permission-gated access to a device's camera and microphone — the part that made "click a link, camera turns on" possible without a plugin, which sounds trivial now and was genuinely not, a decade ago.
Finding a path. Two devices behind two different home networks generally can't just open a direct connection to each other — that's what NAT (network address translation) and firewalls are for, and they're doing their job correctly when they block unsolicited incoming connections. WebRTC's connectivity framework, built around ICE, exists specifically to negotiate a path through that mess. More on this in the next section, because it's worth understanding properly.
Securing the stream. Every WebRTC media stream is encrypted using DTLS-SRTP by default — not an optional setting you have to remember to enable, but baked into the standard itself. DTLS establishes the encryption keys; SRTP (Secure Real-time Transport Protocol) uses them to encrypt the actual audio and video packets in transit. This is why the blanket claim "WebRTC calls aren't encrypted" is simply false for any standards-compliant implementation — transport encryption is there by default, on every call, everywhere.
Adapting to the network in real time. This is the least glamorous and most important part. Real networks are not clean pipes with a fixed, guaranteed bitrate — they're congested, variable, and occasionally hostile, and WebRTC includes congestion control machinery that continuously measures available bandwidth and packet loss, then adjusts what it sends, frame by frame, to match. We'll come back to this too, because it's the single biggest reason a WebRTC call degrades gracefully instead of just freezing.
None of these four pieces is individually revolutionary — encryption, codecs, and congestion control all predate WebRTC by years. What makes WebRTC the thing that actually shipped and won is that it standardized all four together, got every major browser vendor to implement the same standard the same way, and made the whole stack accessible from a few lines of JavaScript. That's the unglamorous, unmarketable achievement underneath the "click a link and you're on a call" experience everyone now takes for granted.

## The Connectivity Problem: ICE, STUN, and TURN

This is the part of WebRTC that does the least visible work and causes the most latency when it's set up wrong, so it deserves a section of its own rather than a passing mention.
Picture two people trying to have a direct phone call, except neither of them has a phone number — both are behind receptionists who won't put through a call unless someone inside the building dialed out first. That's roughly the situation two devices behind two home routers are in. NAT means neither device has a publicly routable address that the other can just dial into; both are hidden behind their router's translation table, which only knows how to route responses to connections it initiated.
STUN (Session Traversal Utilities for NAT) solves the simpler version of this problem. A STUN server sitting on the public internet tells a device, in effect, "here's what your connection looks like from the outside" — the public IP and port your NAT is presenting to the world. Armed with that, two devices can often punch a direct hole through their respective NATs and talk peer-to-peer, with the STUN server stepping out of the way entirely once the connection is established. When this works, it's the fastest possible path — media flows directly between the two browsers with no relay in the middle at all.
It doesn't always work. Some NAT configurations — particularly symmetric NAT, common in stricter corporate and mobile networks — refuse to allow this hole-punching no matter how cleverly you ask. For those cases, TURN (Traversal Using Relays around NAT) is the fallback: a relay server that both sides can reach, which simply forwards traffic between them. It's less efficient — every packet now takes a detour through a third point instead of going directly — but it's a guaranteed connection where direct connectivity fails.
ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment) is the framework that ties these together: it gathers every possible way two devices might reach each other — direct local network paths, STUN-discovered public paths, TURN relay paths — ranks them by preference, and tries them in order until one actually works, falling back gracefully rather than failing outright.
Here's why this matters enormously for private cloud architecture specifically: in a typical two-person call, STUN-assisted direct connection is common and fast. In a multi-tenant office network, behind a corporate firewall, using video conferencing at any real scale, TURN relay is used far more often than most infrastructure teams assume — and every one of those calls is now bottlenecked by wherever that TURN server physically sits. Running your own TURN infrastructure close to your users, rather than depending on whatever default relay a SaaS vendor happens to operate three continents away, is one of the more overlooked latency levers available to a private cloud deployment — and one almost nobody outside the infrastructure team ever thinks to ask about.

## The Architecture That Makes Group Calls Scale: Mesh vs. MCU vs. SFU

Two-person calls are the easy case. The moment a third participant joins, an architectural decision has to be made, and it's the decision that determines whether your ten-person meeting feels smooth or grinds every laptop fan into overdrive.
Mesh topology is the naive approach: every participant connects directly, peer-to-peer, to every other participant. For a two-person call this is exactly the ICE-negotiated direct connection described above, and it's great. For a ten-person call, it means each participant is simultaneously encoding and uploading nine separate video streams — one per peer — which scales catastrophically. Upload bandwidth and CPU load both grow linearly with participant count, which is why mesh architectures fall apart well before ten people, let alone a hundred.
MCU (Multipoint Control Unit) solves the scaling problem by centralizing the work: every participant sends one stream to a central server, which decodes all of them, composites them into a single combined video (think: the classic grid layout), re-encodes that composite, and sends one stream back down to each participant. This dramatically reduces client-side load — each device sends and receives exactly one stream regardless of how many people are on the call — but it comes at a real latency cost, because decoding, compositing, and re-encoding on the server adds processing delay to every single frame, and that server becomes a mandatory bottleneck for every participant's video.
SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit) is the architecture that won, and it's worth understanding exactly why. An SFU receives each participant's stream — just one, same as MCU — but instead of decoding and recompositing it, it simply forwards the encoded streams it receives to the other participants who need them, selecting which streams and which quality layers each recipient actually gets. No decode, no re-encode, no compositing. The server does routing, not media processing.
That distinction is the whole ballgame for latency. An SFU's added delay is close to pure network and queuing time — the minimal processing needed to inspect a packet's header and forward it correctly. An MCU's added delay includes actual video decode-and-re-encode time, which is an order of magnitude more expensive and directly adds to the latency every participant experiences. This is precisely why virtually every serious modern conferencing platform — including the mainstream cloud vendors — runs on SFU architecture for group calls, and it's the architecture worth insisting on for any private cloud deployment as well.
The trade-off SFUs accept in exchange for that speed: client devices still have to decode multiple incoming streams (though only one outgoing), and the SFU can't magically composite a unified view — that compositing work moves to the client, rendering each stream in its own tile. In practice this is the right trade almost everywhere, because client-side decode of several streams is cheap relative to server-side transcoding, and modern displays have no trouble rendering a grid of separately-decoded video tiles.

## The Checklist: What a Low-Latency Private Cloud Deployment Requires

Use the following as an infrastructure checklist. Each item maps to one of the six latency stages described earlier, and each is something you can actually go verify rather than take on faith.
| Requirement | What It Protects Against | What to Verify |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit) Core** | Server-side transcoding delay on every frame | The relay forwards encoded streams without decoding or recompositing |
| **Geographically Proximate Relays** | Long-haul fiber propagation delay | The SFU instance is deployed physically close to participants |
| **Self-Hosted / Co-Located TURN** | Unnecessary hops through distant public TURN relays | You control where TURN relay traffic routes for strict enterprise NATs |
| **Simulcast Enabled** | Forcing all participants down to the weakest connection | Senders publish multiple quality layers; SFU dynamically selects per receiver |
| **Adaptive Congestion Control (GCC)** | Video freezing and audio dropouts | Congestion control smoothly modulates bitrate under network pressure |
| **Calibrated Jitter Buffer** | Choppy playback or artificial delay | Buffer targets adapt to measured jitter rather than static defaults |
| **Live Call Telemetry & Monitoring** | Undetected quality degradation | Track end-to-end RTT, packet loss, and frame delay per session |
A useful test for any private cloud video deployment: if a participant is added in a different country tomorrow, what actually happens to their latency? The honest answer for a well-designed deployment is "the relay nearest to them handles their leg of the call, and the architecture accounts for participants in different regions without funneling everyone through one distant hub." If your honest answer is "everyone connects to the one SFU we run in one region," you have a latency ceiling built into your architecture that no amount of codec tuning will fix.

## Be Precise About What "Low Latency" Actually Means

Just as vendors — including us — should never wave the word "encrypted" over an entire product without specifying which layer it protects, "low latency" deserves the same precision. Different parts of a real deployment affect different stages of delay, and conflating them leads to wasted engineering effort. Here's the honest breakdown of where each lever actually applies:
•	A self-hosted SFU reduces relay delay and, when placed well, network delay — because the physical distance between participants and the server handling their stream shrinks. It does not, by itself, touch encode delay, decode delay, or render delay at all. Self-hosting the relay and expecting it alone to fix a sluggish-feeling call built on an unoptimized encoder is a common and avoidable disappointment.
•	Codec choice affects encode and decode delay, and indirectly bandwidth. Opus for audio is near-universal in WebRTC for good reason — low delay, strong quality even at modest bitrates. On video, newer codecs like AV1 compress more efficiently than VP8 or H.264 at a given quality, but often at real encode-time cost, particularly on lower-powered devices — a genuine trade-off between bandwidth savings and processing latency, not a strictly-better upgrade in every scenario.
•	Congestion control affects how gracefully the system degrades under real network stress, not how fast it is under ideal conditions. Two deployments can have identical latency on a clean network and wildly different experiences the moment someone's Wi-Fi gets flaky — that gap is congestion control doing its job, or not.
•	TURN relay proximity affects the worst-case path, specifically for the participants whose NAT configuration forces relay rather than direct connection. It has zero effect on participants who successfully connect peer-to-peer via STUN, which is exactly why TURN infrastructure is easy to under-invest in — the problem is invisible until you're on the specific network that needs it.
We keep this kind of breakdown specific rather than blanket because "we're low latency" is precisely the sort of claim that's easy to overstate and hard to verify without knowing which of these five independent levers a given architecture actually pulls.

## Where the Real Latency Wins Come From

Encryption controls who can read your media. Architecture controls how fast it moves. For latency specifically, the biggest, most reliable wins come from a short list of decisions — and it's a shorter list than most infrastructure discussions make it sound.
Proximity, above almost everything else. The speed of light in fiber is not a marketing claim — it's a hard physical limit, and no amount of clever software engineering routes around geography. A relay in the same metro region as your participants will beat a relay three continents away every time, regardless of how well-tuned either one's software is. When a private cloud deployment is placed in a single, fixed data center chosen for cost or convenience rather than proximity to actual users, it can genuinely underperform a well-run, geographically distributed SaaS cloud — which is the uncomfortable truth self-hosting advocates don't always volunteer.
SFU architecture, not MCU, for anything beyond a two-person call. As covered above, this is close to a free latency win — SFUs are both faster and cheaper to run than MCUs, with the trade-off landing on the client device rather than the network path.
A relay you actually control, for the participants who need TURN. This is where self-hosting composes cleanly with latency rather than fighting it: owning the TURN infrastructure means you can place it deliberately, close to where your actual users are, instead of inheriting wherever a third-party vendor happened to build theirs.
For deployments spanning multiple regions — a genuinely global organization, not just a company with one office — the strongest posture combines several regional relay deployments with intelligent routing that assigns each call to its nearest instance, rather than one central relay serving the whole world. That's a materially bigger infrastructure commitment than a single self-hosted server, and it's worth being honest that it's not a requirement for every deployment — a single-region private cloud instance is entirely appropriate for an organization whose participants are mostly in one geography, and unnecessary complexity for that case is its own kind of mistake.

## Congestion Control and Simulcast: The Hidden Engines

If proximity and architecture are the big, visible levers, congestion control and simulcast are the quiet ones — invisible when they're working, and the direct cause of "why does this call keep freezing" when they're not.
Congestion control, most commonly implemented via the Google Congestion Control (GCC) algorithm in WebRTC stacks, continuously estimates two things: how much bandwidth is actually available between sender and receiver right now, and how much packet loss and delay variation (jitter) is occurring. Based on those estimates, it adjusts the encoder's target bitrate in near real time — dial it down when the network is struggling, dial it back up when conditions improve. This is precisely why a well-implemented WebRTC call degrades to lower resolution or frame rate under network stress instead of simply freezing or dropping — it's making a continuous, automatic trade-off between quality and delivery, favoring delivery.
Get this tuned too conservatively — reacting slowly to congestion — and users see frozen frames and audio glitches while the algorithm catches up. Tune it too aggressively, and video quality thrashes visibly even on networks that are only mildly imperfect, which is its own kind of annoying. There's no universal correct setting; it depends on the typical network conditions your actual users sit on, which is exactly why real monitoring — not a one-time benchmark — belongs on the checklist above.
Simulcast solves a related but distinct problem: in a group call, participants have wildly different available bandwidth and device capability. Without simulcast, an SFU has one option — forward the same single stream to everyone, which means the whole call gets capped to whatever the weakest participant's connection can handle, a real and common failure mode in mixed-network meetings. With simulcast, each sender encodes and publishes multiple quality layers of their own video simultaneously — say, a low, medium, and high resolution version — and the SFU selects, per receiver, which layer to forward based on that specific receiver's available bandwidth and screen real estate (no point sending a full high-resolution stream to a tile the size of a postage stamp in someone's grid view).
The latency benefit here is subtle but real: because the SFU is choosing between pre-encoded layers rather than requesting a re-encode, it can adapt to a struggling receiver's conditions essentially instantly, without adding any transcoding delay to do so — which is exactly the architectural promise of the SFU model holding up under pressure.

## A Worked Example: One Frame's Journey, Start to Finish

All of the above stays fairly abstract until you follow a single video frame through the entire pipeline, so it's worth doing that once, concretely, with rough numbers attached — not because these exact figures apply to every call, but because seeing where the milliseconds actually go makes the earlier six-stage breakdown click into place.
Say two people are on a call, routed through a well-placed, self-hosted SFU in the same metro region as both of them. A frame from the sender's webcam gets captured by the sensor — call it 5 milliseconds, negligible but not zero. It hits the encoder, which on a reasonably modern device running a well-tuned VP8 or H.264 profile at a sensible resolution might take somewhere in the region of 10–20 milliseconds to compress, longer if the encoder is pushed toward maximum compression efficiency, shorter if it's tuned to favor speed over file size — exactly the trade-off mentioned earlier. The encrypted, encoded frame is packetized and handed to DTLS-SRTP, which wraps it for the wire at negligible additional cost. It then travels across the network to the SFU — with a same-metro relay, this leg might be single-digit milliseconds of pure propagation delay, plus whatever queuing delay exists at each hop, which on a healthy connection is small but on a congested one can dominate everything else in this list combined.
At the SFU, because this is a genuine SFU and not an MCU, the frame isn't decoded or recomposited — it's inspected just enough to route correctly and forwarded, adding perhaps 1–5 milliseconds of processing and queuing time, plus whatever the simulcast layer-selection logic takes to decide which quality tier this particular receiver should get. That forwarded frame crosses the network again to the receiver — another few milliseconds on a same-metro path — arrives, passes back through DTLS-SRTP unwrapping, hits the decoder for another 10–20 milliseconds of processing, and finally sits briefly in the receiver's jitter buffer, typically tuned somewhere in the 20–50 millisecond range, before being rendered to screen.
Add it up on this reasonably favorable, same-metro, well-architected path, and you land somewhere in the neighborhood of 60–120 milliseconds end to end — comfortably inside the 150–200 millisecond threshold where conversation still feels natural, with real margin to spare for the ordinary jitter of a live network.
Now run the same exercise with one variable changed: the SFU sits on a different continent from at least one participant, purely because that's where the infrastructure happened to be provisioned. Propagation delay alone on a genuinely long-haul path can add well over 100 milliseconds each direction before a single router queue or codec decision is even considered — and that number is architecturally fixed by the speed of light in fiber, not something congestion control, simulcast, or a faster CPU can claw back. Every other stage in the pipeline can be as well-tuned as the same-metro example above, and the call will still land close to or past the point where natural turn-taking starts to break down, for the simple reason that one variable — proximity — was never actually optimized. This is the entire argument for treating relay placement as the first infrastructure decision rather than an afterthought: it's the one stage in the pipeline where a bad choice can't be engineered around later, only relocated.

## Private Cloud Done Wrong: Common Self-Hosting Pitfalls

In fairness to the mainstream cloud platforms, and because a comparison this technical should be honest about its own side's failure modes too: self-hosting a WebRTC stack does not automatically produce lower latency, and it's worth naming the specific ways it goes wrong, because every one of them is common.
Single-region deployment for a global user base. Standing up one self-hosted SFU in one data center and routing every participant, everywhere in the world, through it is a straightforward way to build something objectively slower than a well-run multi-region SaaS platform, purely on physical distance grounds. Self-hosting solves a trust problem; it doesn't repeal geography.
Underinvesting in TURN infrastructure. It's easy to get a demo working with STUN-only connectivity on a friendly network and assume the job is done, then discover in production that a meaningful share of real-world participants — anyone behind strict corporate NAT — are silently falling back to a TURN relay that was never properly provisioned or placed, adding exactly the kind of latency the whole project was meant to avoid.
Under-provisioned relay capacity. An SFU handling far more concurrent streams than the hardware and network link were sized for will introduce queuing delay indistinguishable, from the user's perspective, from network congestion — the fix is capacity planning, not encryption or architecture.
Treating deployment as a one-time project instead of an operated service. Codecs get updated, congestion control algorithms improve, client libraries patch real bugs — a self-hosted stack that isn't actively maintained tends to drift from "as fast as it could be" toward "acceptable, barely," slowly enough that nobody notices until a new hire on a bad connection asks why the call feels sluggish.
None of this is an argument against private cloud deployment — it's an argument for taking it seriously as an ongoing engineering responsibility rather than a one-time infrastructure checkbox. The organizations that get real latency benefits from self-hosting are the ones that treat relay placement, capacity, and monitoring as first-class, continuously-owned concerns, not the ones that stood up a server once and moved on.

## Putting It Into Practice: Engineering Blueprint

A pragmatic rollout for an infrastructure team evaluating or building a private cloud WebRTC deployment looks like this:
1.	Map your participants to geography before choosing infrastructure. If your organization is genuinely global, plan for multiple regional relay deployments from the start; if it's concentrated in one region, a single well-placed instance is the right-sized answer, and over-building multi-region infrastructure for a single-office company is its own waste.
2.	Confirm SFU architecture, and confirm it specifically — don't take "we use WebRTC" as a proxy for "we use an SFU"; ask directly whether group calls are relayed or transcoded, because the answer changes your latency ceiling by an order of magnitude.
3.	Provision and place TURN deliberately, rather than accepting a default. Test connectivity from the actual network environments your users sit on — corporate VPNs, hotel Wi-Fi, mobile carriers — not just a clean office connection, because that's where TURN reliance shows up.
4.	Enable simulcast and verify it's actually negotiated, not just theoretically supported by the library you're using — client and server both need to agree to it, and it's a common integration gap.
5.	Monitor real call quality metrics continuously, not just uptime. Round-trip time, packet loss, and jitter per call tell you things a healthy-looking server dashboard won't.
6.	Revisit the deployment on a cadence, treating it as an operated service with an owner, not a completed project — because the ways self-hosting backfires above are almost all slow-drift failures, not sudden ones.

## The Bottom Line

Low-latency video conferencing isn't the product of one clever setting or one impressive-sounding architecture decision — it's the sum of six separate delay stages, each with its own bottleneck and its own fix, held together by a genuinely well-engineered open standard in WebRTC that handles connectivity, security, and network adaptation as a single coherent package rather than three separate afterthoughts.
Moving that stack onto private cloud infrastructure is a real opportunity to control the variable that matters most for latency — how close the relay sits to the people using it — but it's an opportunity, not a guarantee. Done with real attention to SFU architecture, TURN placement, simulcast, and honest ongoing monitoring, a private cloud deployment can match or beat a distant multi-tenant cloud on raw speed while also keeping the infrastructure inside a boundary you actually control. Done as a one-time server standup with no ongoing ownership, it can just as easily end up slower than the SaaS platform it replaced. The difference between those two outcomes isn't the technology — WebRTC is the same open standard either way — it's whether the team running it treats latency as an engineering discipline or a checkbox.
