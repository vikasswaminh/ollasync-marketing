A multilingual meeting with **100 people actually in the room** is not the same purchase as a meeting for a 100-person company. The former has a hard attendance constraint: if the organizer, interpreters, presenters, and guests all count toward the limit, a plan capped at 100 has no margin for one late arrival. The latter might need only one paid organizer license, or it might require many licensed employees; neither can be assumed from headcount alone. Language delivery adds a second constraint: do people need to **read translated captions**, **hear translated speech**, or speak through a **human interpreter**? Those are different experiences and different procurement questions.




This article compares one concrete scenario: a scheduled, interactive, 90-minute meeting; 100 intended simultaneous participants including the organizer; several speakers; attendees who want to follow in different languages; and a requirement to decide what to do with the recording afterward. It is **not** a ranking of raw translation accuracy, a guarantee of availability in every region, or a quote for 100 subscriptions. Product documentation and public plan descriptions were checked on **November 10, 2026**; availability, packaging, and local prices can change. Confirm the exact tenant, billing country, and contract before committing.




The short answer: **Zoom** has documented translated captions and a mature meeting workflow; determine the relevant entitlement and whether captions or interpretation meet your needs. **Teams** supports live translated captions under eligible licensing, including an organizer-license route that can cover participants; existing Microsoft 365 deployment may simplify administration. **Google Meet** offers translated captions *and* documented speech translation on eligible plans, but speech translation has a published per-meeting usage limit on several editions. **Ollasync** offers individual translated captions and optional spoken translated audio on its published 100-person Pro and Business meetings; because 100 is its published ceiling, do not choose it for a meeting that might exceed 100 without a separately confirmed capacity arrangement. No platform should be selected solely from this summary: run the exact event rehearsal below.




For general product positioning see the [comparison hub](/vs/). The existing [Zoom, Webex, Teams and Ollasync comparison](/blog/compare-zoom-webex-microsoft-teams-and-ollasync/) covers broader enterprise architecture, and the [Meet, Zoom and Ollasync cost comparison](/blog/google-meet-vs-zoom-vs-ollasync-2026-cost-comparison/) tackles broad purchasing arguments. Neither answers the narrower question of **100 simultaneous people, multilingual participation, plan gates and operational headroom**. ## Define the meeting before comparing plans




Write a one-page brief with the number of people who will **join at once**, not the number invited. Include the host, co-host, speakers, accessibility support, interpretation staff, and external guests in your count unless the vendor explicitly documents an exception. If exactly 100 must join and the plan says â€œup to 100,â€ treat 100 as a hard stop, not a comfortable target. Ask whether organizers joining from a second device count as another participant. If the audience may climb to 105, price and rehearse a tier that supports **more than 100**; do not assume a 100-cap product can absorb the extra five.




Next separate two language paths. In a *caption-first* meeting, attendees can listen to the source audio while reading text in their preferred language. This may work well when attendees can keep their eyes on a screen and tolerate some reading. In a *listen-first* meeting, an attendee needs the translated speech delivered to their ears while looking at a demo, slides, or a speaker. A product offering translated captions should not be represented as offering automatic spoken translation on that basis alone. A third path is human simultaneous interpretation: it can be preferable for legal, medical, labor, or safety-critical statements where a qualified person and a correction procedure are required. Check how each product handles interpretation channels if that is your actual requirement; do not substitute machine captions by default.

## Zoom: translated captions are real; verify the purchased entitlement




Zoomâ€™s own [translated-captions explanation](https://www.zoom.com/en/blog/translated-captions/) describes the feature in Meetings and Webinars, says users can select their own speaking language, and describes it as included in named Zoom Workplace higher tiers or available as an add-on for paid accounts. Its [virtual meetings page](https://www.zoom.com/en/products/virtual-meetings/) also advertises multilingual caption translations. That is evidence **for** a usable translation path, not evidence that a standard Zoom meeting is English-only or that every multilingual meeting requires third-party software. Zoom may be a strong default if employees and external speakers already use it fluently and the purchased entitlement includes the language feature.







Distinguish **captions** from **spoken audio**. The cited Zoom translated-captions page establishes text translation; it does not settle whether a separately offered Zoom audio-translation capability fits the buyerâ€™s plan, language pair, geography, or meeting configuration. Zoom also supports other language workflows, including human interpretation; evaluate those against the same brief instead of assuming that captions are the only available Zoom language mechanism. If the team needs translated speech, ask Zoom for the specific product documentation, entitlement, language list, delivery mode and a demonstration in your own tenant. Until that is done, spoken automatic translation for this scenario remains **not confirmed** in the table.

## Meet: distinguish translated captions from speech translation




Google has two documented paths, and conflating them leads to bad purchasing advice. Its [translated captions help page](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/10964115?hl=en) lists eligible Workspace editions and supported caption languages. Its separate [Speech Translation help page](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/16221730?hl=en) describes real-time translated **spoken** output: participants select a language to hear, speakers must consent to having their speech translated, and listeners may hear translation a few seconds after the source. Google says the feature can be used by everyone in a scheduled meeting when its host has an eligible plan. Those are stronger claims than â€œMeet only offers subtitles.â€




The speech page also lists a **90-minute per-meeting usage limit** on several eligible plans, including Workspace Business Standard and Plus and Enterprise Standard and Plus; other listed options have an eight-hour limit. This example meeting is deliberately 90 minutes: a plan with a 90-minute speech allowance is at the edge before introductions run long, a speaker joins late, or a technical interruption consumes time. The help article describes a usage limit *per meeting*, not a guaranteed uninterrupted 90-minute interpretation window. If the spoken-language experience must continue beyond the scheduled end, seek an eligible longer-limit plan or an approved fallback. Check the live documentation and your administratorâ€™s configuration, since rollouts and eligibility can change.




Capacity is a separate gate. Googleâ€™s [edition comparison](https://support.google.com/a/answer/10037875?hl=en) lists **100** participants on Business Starter, **150** on Business Standard, and **500** on Business Plus. Starter may fit 100 people numerically but offers no spare capacity; moreover, Googleâ€™s translated-caption and speech-translation eligibility lists do **not** simply follow meeting capacity. If the desired language feature requires Standard or Plus, compare those actual editions. A 150-cap Standard meeting affords headroom against modest attendance overrun, but confirm who counts and whether your organization is purchasing that edition in its region. Do not infer that the cheapest 100-cap option includes every translation feature.

## Run one fair rehearsal before approving the purchase




Schedule a 20- to 30-minute pilot using the *same* agenda fragment on each platform. Make the host the same person where possible and invite representative external guests. Include at least one attendee on a mobile device, one on a weaker connection, one who reads captions, and one who requires translated speech. Where a product only evidences captions, assess that path honestly; do not mark it as a failed speech test for a feature you have not licensed or researched. Ask a fluent bilingual participant to judge whether important meaning survived, not to assign a made-up percentage accuracy score.










Set a fallback before the real event. Designate a co-host who can post essential decisions in chat, a second access route for attendees who cannot use translated speech, and a person who can pause a high-risk discussion for clarification. If language failure would invalidate the meeting, use a qualified human interpreter and an agreed correction procedure. For a meeting at a hard 100 cap, also establish a registration cutoff or choose a larger-cap plan. Good procurement includes recovery time and a capacity buffer; it does not rely on a vendor slogan.




## Region, privacy, and procurement checks




â€œAvailable in 19 languagesâ€ or â€œsupports translated captionsâ€ is not a statement about where audio is processed. Before scheduling a sensitive meeting, ask **each** vendor or reseller for the contracting entity, applicable data-processing agreement, hosting and processing regions for live media and AI features, subprocessors, retention controls, guest access policies, and whether region selection applies to the translation service as well as storage. If a required region cannot be established in writing for the purchased plan, record **not confirmed** and do not infer compliance from the language UI. Review the buyerâ€™s own legal requirements; this article is not legal advice.




Separate transport encryption, storage encryption and end-to-end encryption. A provider can protect data in transit while still processing audio to generate captions or speech. An E2EE mode can disable server-side language features. Ask for a demo of the *same* security mode in which the event will run, not a translated meeting in one mode and a security screenshot from another. Where legal or contractual rules require a particular treatment, involve the organizationâ€™s security and privacy owners before the pilot. Make the accessibility owner a reviewer too: translated captions support multilingual reading, but are not automatically an adequate replacement for accessibility services.

## FAQ: the questions a 100-person host should ask




### Does a 100-person meeting mean I must purchase 100 licenses?




No general rule supports that conclusion. Participants may be guests while licenses attach to organizers or employees; eligibility also depends on the translation feature and plan. Microsoft documents a case in which an eligible organizer allows participants to use translated captions without each holding that specific license. Google documents eligible-host Speech Translation for scheduled meetings. Ollasync publishes per-seat host plans and meeting limits. Zoom entitlements vary by plan or add-on. Ask each vendor to identify the **named licensed users** required for the exact event and confirm guest behavior in a trial.




### Can 100 invitees join Ollasync Pro or Business with headroom?




The [published plans](/pricing/) say **up to 100 people** on both Pro and Business; they do not advertise headroom above 100. Count the organizer among intended joiners for planning unless the product confirms another rule. If every slot is planned to be occupied, a last-minute additional participant is a real risk. Business changes translated-voice allowance and host concurrency; its published participant cap remains 100. Get a separately documented arrangement or choose a verified higher-cap meeting if overflow is plausible.




### Does Zoom offer translated captions, or would I need an outside interpreter?




Zoom [documents translated captions](https://www.zoom.com/en/blog/translated-captions/) in Meetings and Webinars; its article describes inclusion on certain higher tiers and an add-on for paid accounts. An outside interpreter is **not inherently required** for translated captions. Whether human interpretation is preferable for a consequential discussion is a different decision. Confirm the purchased Zoom entitlement, the attendeesâ€™ target languages, and any separately required spoken-language experience rather than assuming Zoom has no native language tools.




### Are Meet translated captions and Speech Translation the same thing?




No. [Translated captions](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/10964115?hl=en) display translated text; [Speech Translation](https://support.google.com/meet/answer/16221730?hl=en) can deliver translated speech on eligible plans and has its own usage limits and consent behavior. A plan that can host 100 people does not automatically include both. Ask the administrator which edition is actually assigned to the host, then rehearse with attendees who need each mode.




### Will guests hear every translation in the recording?




Do not assume so. Google says its Speech Translation is not available in recordings. Zoomâ€™s translated-caption article distinguishes live captions from the recording and mentions transcript behavior. Teams says live captions are not themselves saved and directs users to transcription when a transcript is needed. Ollasync distinguishes participant-selected local recording from a room-level cloud recording. Specify the exact replay format and inspect a pilot export before promising a multilingual archive.




### Which is cheapest for our 100-person, 90-minute event?




**Not confirmed** without your existing subscriptions, host license counts, target-language mode, country, currency, billing term, usage and quotes. A 100-person cap can create a capacity risk even when its sticker price looks low. A license already owned may have little incremental event cost while still representing an annual platform commitment. Use the worksheet above and compare both incremental event spending and ongoing program cost under a common scope.




### What should we do if translation is essential for a sensitive meeting?




Test the actual accents, languages, devices and terminology with bilingual reviewers; include a pause-and-correct process and a backup channel. Ask security owners to verify processing region, data protection terms and whether the selected security mode permits language features. Where mistakes could cause material harm, arrange qualified interpretation and review rather than claiming that any machine-translation product guarantees accuracy.
