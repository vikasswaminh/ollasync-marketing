**Short answer:** Treat the conference as a private conversation about one student, not a translated classroom event. Verify the invited adults and their access under district procedures; ask the family what language, interpreter, disability-related access, device, and timing they need; and send a plain-language invitation that does not disclose sensitive details. Schedule a qualified interpreter if required or needed for meaningful discussion. Test the actual call, hold one speaker at a time, and pause for interpretation. Do not record or use an unapproved translation service by default. Restate each proposed decision in both languages, check the family's understanding without testing their English, and send a concise, reviewed follow-up through an approved school channel.

This guide is for the individual online family conference: a teacher, a parent or other authorized adult, and perhaps an approved interpreter or specialist discussing a particular child. It is not a guide to delivering multilingual lessons to a class. The existing [accessible multilingual virtual classroom guide](/blog/best-practices-for-accessible-multilingual-virtual-classrooms/) covers student-facing instruction; the [bilingual business meeting runbook](/blog/how-do-i-run-a-bilingual-business-meeting-without-a-human-interpreter/) addresses lower-risk commercial discussions. Neither determines who may hear a child's educational information or how a school should communicate with that child's family. School staff should follow district policy and ask the district's privacy, language-access, and accessibility leads when a case is uncertain. This is an operational checklist, not a legal determination about a particular family.

## What must the school establish before it sends a link?

Start with the meeting type. An ordinary progress check can cover strengths, concerns, and a date to revisit them. A formal evaluation, discipline, or specialized-service meeting may have separate notice and participation rules: involve the responsible school team rather than using a routine conference template.

Confirm the adults who may attend using current school records and district procedure. **Do not decide rights from a surname, a shared home, an email address, or a teacher's memory.** Under [FERPA's regulations](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/ferpa), parents have rights relating to education records; the rules address rights of both custodial and noncustodial parents, subject to a court order, state statute, or other legally binding document that specifically revokes them. Rights generally transfer to an eligible student at age 18 or upon attendance at a postsecondary institution, with regulatory exceptions. Those principles are not a meeting-by-meeting attendee list. A district must check the records and applicable process for this student before deciding whom to invite or what can be disclosed. Where the student is an eligible student, ask the district how participation and any disclosure authorization should work.

An everyday caregiver, guardian, or requested support person may have a different role. Ask the office that maintains access restrictions rather than adjudicating documents on a live call. If records conflict, defer sensitive discussion. Do not reveal another adult's private circumstances to someone whose access is in doubt.

Use an attendee list with roles rather than a broad calendar group: teacher, authorized parent or eligible student, approved interpreter, and any specialist whose participation is appropriate to the subject. If the meeting involves two households, do not assume a joint call is safe or appropriate; ask the school to determine whether separate invitations or conferences are needed. Use a student-specific link and a neutral invitation subject line without a diagnosis, disciplinary status, or grades.

## How should the school learn what the family needs?

Contact the family through an approved method that the school already uses, preferably with a person or translated message the family can understand. Ask, rather than infer, which language they prefer for speaking, listening, and written follow-up. Those may differ. Ask whether they want a professional interpreter, translated text, same-language captions, a phone connection, a device-accessible document, extra time to read, or another accommodation. Someone who speaks conversational English may still prefer an interpreter when discussing assessment terminology or a consequential decision. No family should have to justify that preference by demonstrating inadequate English on camera.

A scheduling clerk does not need the child's progress report to book an interpreter. Privately ask: “Would you like an interpreter? What language and variety? Do you need captions or another way to join? What time works?” Avoid a group thread revealing family circumstances, and offer a phone response route.

[W3C's accessible-events checklist](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/) recommends anticipating different abilities, offering accessible materials, and being open to needs that were not predicted in advance. For a school, this means checking the exact device and join route the family intends to use rather than declaring a platform “accessible” because it has a captions icon. A parent may join on a basic phone, use a screen reader, rely on audio, or share a device in a noisy home. Ask what actually helps; offer an alternative route if the first choice does not work. The district's accessibility team should decide any required accommodation and provider arrangements.

A language preference is not the same as a disability accommodation. [W3C distinguishes same-language captions from translated subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/): captions represent speech and relevant sounds in the spoken language, whereas translated subtitles carry speech in another language. Translated text may help a parent follow the teacher but will not necessarily meet the needs of someone requiring accessible live captions; likewise captions in English will not by themselves interpret the conversation into Spanish. Ask about both, without substituting one for the other.

### What if the family does not respond?

Silence is not a choice of English or a refusal of support. Try approved alternate outreach routes and document attempts in the school system. Offer a phone or in-person option if available. If formal notice deadlines apply, escalate rather than assuming a voicemail satisfies them. Never share the child's situation with a class group to find the family.

## How should the invitation and agenda be written?

Send a phone-readable invitation early enough to arrange support. Use reviewed language the family understands; route formal wording through the district's approved translation process. A machine-generated draft is not an official translation of procedural rights.

A practical invitation identifies the teacher and school, the date and local time with time zone, expected duration, private joining instructions, purpose in general terms, invited roles, interpreter or caption arrangements, and a direct contact for changes. Say whether live automated translation is proposed and how the family can request another mode. Explain the plan for recording separately; the simplest default for an individual student conference is no recording and no retained AI transcript unless district procedure has approved a specific need and process. Never promise absolute secrecy or imply that merely clicking the link approves every use of meeting data.

A sample adaptable invitation follows. It is **not** a statutory notice or a translation into another language:

> Hello. I would like to meet with you about your child's learning and next steps. We can meet on Tuesday at 3:30 p.m. [school time zone] for about 25 minutes by private video link or by phone. An interpreter in [requested language] can join if you would like one; please tell us if you need captions or another way to participate. Our planned participants are [roles, not private third-party details]. We do not plan to record. Please reply to [school contact] to confirm the time and your preferred way to join, or suggest another time.

Send the actual student-specific link directly to confirmed recipients through an approved channel, not inside a public calendar description or class newsletter. Do not put grades, behavior incidents, or attachments containing student data in an unverified SMS or translation site merely to make the invitation feel complete. If the school provides a portal, use the approved portal for sensitive documents. Confirm the family's contact address through the normal process; a mistyped email can disclose more than a poor translation.

Make the agenda concrete and bounded: (1) language and sound check, (2) teacher shares strengths, (3) one or two specific concerns and evidence, (4) family perspective and questions, (5) proposed next steps, (6) readback and follow-up date. Provide a brief glossary in advance for school-specific terms such as “reading benchmark” or “missing work,” but do not distribute other students' data or a full roster to help a translator learn names. If a separate formal meeting is needed, the agenda should say the present call is informational only and identify the school contact for the formal process.

### How long should the school book?

Allow time for two directions of interpretation and questions. Shorten the agenda or lengthen the slot instead of rushing. State the time zone, confirm the interpreter received the link, and designate someone to handle a provider no-show. A pre-brief can clarify terminology, not authorize the interpreter to decide for the family.

## Which translation or interpretation mode should the school choose?

Choose the mode according to the meeting's stakes, participants' needs, and district rules, not the most impressive demo. A **qualified human interpreter** can ask for clarification and preserve turn-taking; an automated system may mishear names, negate a statement, or flatten a parent's concern. Automated translation can sometimes help with routine, low-risk exchange, but it is not an interpreter credential and should not be called “professional interpretation.” If the meeting requires a qualified interpreter under applicable district policy or other requirements, arrange one; do not substitute AI because it is available in the meeting app. Ask the district's language-access lead what standards apply to a formal or consequential conversation.

| Mode | Useful when | What to check before use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human interpreter on the call | A required service, a sensitive or consequential discussion, complex questions, or a family's request | Approved provider, correct language and dialect, confidentiality arrangements, audio path, turn-taking, backup |
| Same-language live captions | A participant benefits from reading speech or requires an accessible captioning arrangement | Accuracy, speaker identification, relevant sound information, device display, district-approved captioning method |
| Translated text | A family prefers reading translated speech for a lower-risk discussion | Both language directions, readability on a phone, lag, mistakes, whether text is stored |
| Translated audio | A participant prefers listening rather than reading | Supported language pair, separation of original and translated voice, latency, volume, interruption handling |
| Phone plus interpreter | Video or broadband is a barrier | Verified phone number, private place to speak, clear three-way connection, no voicemail disclosure |
| Reviewed written exchange | A point cannot be safely settled live | Who reviews the translation, secure delivery, response opportunity, deadline if any |

These are possible arrangements, **not claims that one product includes every mode**. A particular platform's available languages, permissions, caption behavior, and storage depend on its configuration. Do not promise features until school IT tests them with the actual account and device. For a general comparison of listening and reading choices, see [translated audio versus captions](/blog/should-my-multilingual-meeting-use-translated-audio-or-captions/); the school's first question remains whether the mode is approved and adequate for this family and this subject.

Avoid using a child, sibling, classmate, or unapproved family member as the default interpreter. They may miss technical language, feel pressure to filter an adult conversation, or hear sensitive information that should not be shared with them. A parent may prefer a support person, but the school still needs to check attendance and confidentiality under district procedures. An interpreter's role is to convey meaning, not to decide what the teacher should say or to answer for the family. Address the parent directly, allow complete interpretation, and ask the interpreter to flag an unclear term rather than guess.

### Can automated translation help with an ordinary check-in?

Possibly, with district approval and a clear explanation of limits. Test both speaking directions, including questions, names, numbers, negatives, and school terms; use invented examples rather than student records. The [live-translation rehearsal guide](/blog/how-to-test-live-translation-accuracy-before-a-meeting/) outlines a general test. Fluent translated audio can still change “could” into “will.” Explain how to request clarification. If translation is unreliable, move to an approved interpreter or reschedule; automated captions do not replace a required human interpreter.

## What privacy checks belong in the technical rehearsal?

The school should verify that its chosen meeting and any speech-processing tools are approved for the intended student data. Do not claim that a service is “FERPA-certified”: the [U.S. Department of Education's FERPA regulations](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/ferpa) describe duties and permitted disclosures, not a universal certification badge for meeting products. Ask district IT and privacy staff which accounts, provider contracts, retention settings, and access controls are approved. A marketing statement about security does not decide whether this specific use and recipient list are allowed.

Under the Department's [definition of education records](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/what-education-record), records directly related to a student and maintained by a school or a party acting for it can take many forms, including audio, video, email, and computer media. Do not assume a saved conference recording, transcript, or generated summary is harmless merely because it came from a video service. Whether a particular artifact qualifies and how it may be retained or shared require context and district review. Minimize generated artifacts: leave recording, transcription, AI notes, and automatic sharing off unless expressly approved for this meeting; check actual settings rather than trusting a default label.

Test admission controls, names, interpreter access, and screen sharing with fictional data. A forwarded link or password does not establish identity; check each attendee through district procedure, without reading identifiers aloud to strangers. Never publish the link.

Close unrelated browser tabs and school records before sharing a screen. Open only the child's approved materials; remove other names from comparison charts, attendance sheets, classroom screenshots, and notifications. Aggregate context may be helpful, but a gradebook showing classmates is not. If another student appears unexpectedly in a shared file, stop screen-sharing, correct the display, and follow the district's incident process if information was disclosed. The same caution applies to chat: avoid pasting a full student record into a meeting chat that may persist beyond the call.

Check whether everyone can speak privately; offer a different time if the family is in a shared space. Do not require a view of their home. Audio may suffice where district procedure permits.

## How should the teacher conduct the live conversation?

Open with names and roles: “I am the teacher. [Name] is our interpreter. We are here to discuss [student's] learning and agree on next steps.” Verify the intended adult is present by the district's approved method, and confirm the family can hear and use the agreed language or access support. Ask whether anyone else is listening on either end before discussing specifics; if an unexpected guest is present, pause and resolve access rather than debating rights in front of that person. Explain whether any recording or transcript is active. If the configuration differs from the invitation, stop and fix it or reschedule before sharing student information.

Speak to the parent, not about the parent to the interpreter. Use short complete thoughts, one speaker at a time. Pause after a question or data point so the interpreter can finish. If using translated captions or speech, allow additional reading or listening time. Describe a chart verbally and identify the exact date, assignment, or observation rather than saying “you can see it there.” [W3C's guidance for accessible events](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/) recommends clear microphones, accessible materials, describing pertinent visuals, and reviewing important points. Those habits also make correction easier when a translated phrase is wrong.

Begin with strengths and context, then discuss one concern at a time. Distinguish observation (“Three assignments are marked missing in the school's record as of Monday”) from interpretation (“We think motivation is the issue”). Ask the family what they see at home without treating their response as a cross-examination. A parent may have information about access to devices, work schedules, or the child's experience that changes the plan. Give the interpreter time to convey the full answer. Do not allow a staff member to paraphrase a parent's translated concern as agreement with a preferred intervention.

For any proposal, identify what is a suggestion, what has already been decided under school procedure, and what needs further review. Use dates and owners: “The teacher will provide two reading passages by Friday; the family will tell us whether the proposed practice time is workable; we will check progress in two weeks.” If the meeting enters a topic with a different formal process, state that no decision on that topic is being made in this routine call and name the follow-up office. The facilitator can maintain a short list of open questions without recording everything said.

### What is a respectful way to confirm understanding?

Do not ask “Do you understand English?” or treat a polite “yes” as evidence that a translated plan was accurate. Instead, repeat each action in plain language, ask the interpreter to convey it in full, and invite the family to describe what they expect to happen next: “I want to make sure I explained our plan clearly. What would you expect from us by Friday?” The teacher should also summarize the parent's point back and allow correction. This checks the school's explanation as much as the family's comprehension.

Pause on ambiguous numbers, names, deadlines, and negatives. If the parent says the child **cannot** attend a particular session, do not let a missing “not” create a commitment. Write the disputed sentence down in a school-approved place, ask for a fresh interpretation or reviewed translation, and avoid labeling it a decision until both sides confirm. “We need to clarify this before acting” is a useful outcome. Do not pressure the family into immediate agreement merely because the video slot is ending.

### What if the interpreter disconnects or captions fail?

Stop substantive discussion. Keep a neutral script ready: “Our language support stopped. We will pause and reconnect or arrange another time. No decision is being made while we cannot communicate reliably.” Send a brief approved message in the family's preferred language if possible. Do not continue with academic details using improvised translation from another attendee. If the caption feed fails for a participant who needs it, the same pause rule applies; a translation subtitle is not automatically a substitute for accessible captions. If connection quality makes every second sentence uncertain, move to a supported phone call or reschedule.

If an unauthorized guest joins or a screen accidentally exposes another student's information, stop sharing or end the call, preserve only the facts needed for the district's response, and promptly follow the district's incident procedure. Do not try to solve a potential privacy incident by deleting an unofficial chat log and saying nothing. The parent still deserves a properly supported follow-up meeting; the privacy response and the educational conversation are separate tasks.

## What should happen after everyone leaves?

Send a limited, readable recap through a district-approved route to the person authorized to receive it. Include the agreed actions, each owner, deadlines, unanswered questions, and next contact date. Have a qualified person review any consequential translated wording before it goes out; distinguish “proposed” from “approved.” Give the family an easy way to correct a misunderstanding in the language they prefer. Do not automatically email a full transcript or AI-generated summary to every attendee. The goal is to preserve the actionable result, not to create the largest possible record of a sensitive conversation.

Keep notes under district retention and access rules. Ask IT whether a vendor or interpreter created a temporary artifact and how it is handled. Avoid personal cloud folders or downloaded home recordings. Do not promise to erase an official record or retain an automatic transcript without the responsible office's review.

Check whether the agreed action actually occurred. A teacher can send the promised learning material, mark the next check-in date, and confirm that the family received a comprehensible follow-up. If the family replies that the written plan differs from what they heard, reopen the question instead of treating the recap as conclusive. A follow-up is not proof of agreement simply because it was sent. Where a formal process has separate documentation or procedural rights, route the matter back to its assigned school team rather than substituting this informal recap.

## What can staff use as a day-of-meeting checklist?

| When | School action | Stop or escalate if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before booking | Verify student, meeting type, attendee access, family contact route, and district-approved language/access arrangements | Rights or procedure are unclear; a formal process applies |
| Before inviting | Ask the family privately about language, interpreter, captions, device, time, and alternative join method | The family cannot understand the invitation or requested support is unavailable |
| Before the call | Confirm interpreter booking and backup, test both speech directions with fictional content, secure the link, disable unapproved capture | Provider is missing, guest access is uncontrolled, or retention settings are unknown |
| At admission | Check names and roles, language and caption access, privacy at each end, and any active recording | An unexpected participant joins or the agreed access route fails |
| During discussion | One speaker at a time; share only this student's approved materials; interpret questions and read back actions | Translation changes meaning or another student's data appears |
| Afterward | Send a reviewed, limited recap through approved channels and track open questions | Recap is disputed, a disclosure occurred, or a formal decision requires another process |

Build a repeatable school workflow: name a scheduling contact, interpreter-booking route, phone fallback, privacy escalation contact, and approved place for follow-up. Rehearse an interpreter no-show and unexpected caller using fictional data. Staff must know when to pause, not just how to start a call.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can two parents in different households join the same call?

Sometimes, but the teacher should not decide solely from a shared student relationship. Verify each adult's access and any restrictions through the district's process, ask whether a joint call is appropriate, and issue individual joining instructions if approved. A noncustodial parent is not automatically excluded under [FERPA's parent-rights provisions](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/ferpa), nor is every participant automatically entitled to every piece of information in a particular meeting. A disputed or sensitive arrangement belongs with the district's designated decision-maker, not in a live discussion with both households already connected.

### Can the student attend their own conference?

Ask the school team: age, purpose, and formal procedures matter. A student may contribute to goal-setting but should not serve as the parent's interpreter. FERPA rights may have transferred to an eligible student; do not assume the usual parent attendee arrangement still applies.

### Do captions replace an interpreter for a family that requests one?

Not automatically. Same-language captions display speech in the original language; translated subtitles present a translation and can omit or mistranslate meaning. [W3C explains this distinction and the limits of automatic captions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/). A family may need both captions and a human interpreter. Confirm the actual access requirement with the district and the family, rather than making the parent choose between understanding the teacher and reading the conversation accessibly.

### Should every conference be recorded to help with translation later?

No. Recording creates an additional artifact with access, retention, and disclosure questions; it does not guarantee an accurate interpretation. Use a limited, reviewed action recap by default under district policy. If recording is genuinely needed for a specific purpose, have the district approve the process, notify participants appropriately, check the platform's actual capture settings, and provide the required access arrangement. A recording should never be a workaround for a missing live interpreter when meaningful real-time participation is needed.

### Can a parent bring an interpreter or support person?

Welcome the request, clarify the person's role, and check district attendee rules before the call. Ask whether an approved school interpreter is also needed. A support person is not automatically qualified to interpret specialized terms; settle this privately before admitting guests.

### Can the school use one multilingual link for all families on conference night?

Use a separate student-specific meeting or equivalent access boundary for each private conference. A reusable classroom or open-house link makes it too easy for the next family to enter early or for a forwarded link to expose student information. If the school runs a general orientation or open house, keep it separate from individual student discussions. A school-wide event's language channels and audience-management needs differ from the privacy and attendee checks of a family conference.

### What if the family's language is not supported?

Escalate to the district's language-access lead. Confirm the precise language, seek an approved interpreter or alternate time, and tell the family when the school will update them. Do not call an unsupported automatic mode “close enough” or use the child as interpreter. A properly supported later appointment is better than an on-time call that the family cannot safely use.
